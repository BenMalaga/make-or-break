A support chatbot gets asked, "Can I return shoes I bought three weeks ago?"
It answers, confidently, "Returns are accepted within 14 days of purchase."
The real policy is 30 days. Nobody wrote 14 anywhere. The model made it up.

This is not a bug in the usual sense. As how-language-models-work laid out, the
model predicts the next token from patterns it absorbed during training, and it
never read this company's handbook. The handbook is private, or it was written
after the model's training cutoff, or both. Asked about it anyway, the model
produces text that sounds like a return policy, because sounding right is the
only thing it was trained to do. It has no way to know the real number.

Retraining the model on the handbook is possible but slow, costly, and stale the
day the policy changes. There is a cheaper move. Find the exact passage of the
handbook that answers the question, and paste it into the prompt right before
asking. The model reads the real policy and answers from it. That trick, done
properly, is called retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, and it is how most
real AI products give a model knowledge it was never trained on.

## Why not just paste everything in

The obvious version of this idea is to skip the "find the passage" step and drop
the whole handbook into the prompt. building-on-the-api showed how code sends a
prompt to the model and gets a response, so pasting 200 pages of text ahead of
the question is one string away. For a single short document, that is genuinely
the right call, and it is simpler than everything below. The trouble starts when
the knowledge is large.

Every model reads a bounded amount of text at once, the context window from
how-language-models-work, measured in tokens. A company wiki, a codebase, or a
year of support tickets is far larger than any window, even the large ones that
hold hundreds of thousands of tokens. It simply does not fit.

Cost and speed are the next wall. The API charges per token on every single
call, and a bigger prompt takes longer to process. Sending 200 pages with every
question means paying for 200 pages every question, and waiting longer for each
answer, to use one paragraph of it.

The quiet problem is quality. A model does not attend evenly across a very long
prompt. A relevant fact buried in the middle of 100,000 tokens of noise gets
found less reliably than the same fact sitting alone at the top. Researchers call
this the "lost in the middle" effect, and it means more context can make an
answer worse, not better. So the better move is to send the model only the few
passages that actually bear on the question. Everything in this lesson is
machinery for finding those passages.

## An embedding turns meaning into numbers

Finding "the passages that bear on the question" is the hard part, because the
right passage often shares no words with the question. Someone asks "how do I get
my money back," and the answer lives under a heading that says "Refunds." A plain
keyword search, matching identical words, misses it. What is needed is a way to
match on meaning.

An embedding is how a machine measures meaning. An embedding model, a neural
network that is a close cousin of the language model, reads a piece of text and
outputs a fixed-length list of numbers called a vector. The same text always
produces the same vector. "Refund policy" might come out as a list starting
`[0.02, -0.19, 0.44, ...]`, and it is the same list every time.

The point of those numbers is that they encode meaning. Two texts that mean
similar things get vectors that sit close together, and two unrelated texts get
vectors far apart. "How do I get my money back" and "Refunds are issued to the
original card" land near each other even with no shared words, because the
embedding model learned, from enormous amounts of text, that those phrases travel
in the same company.

A real embedding vector is not three numbers, it is a few hundred to a few
thousand: common models output 384, 1,536, or 3,072 of them. Each number is one
coordinate, so the vector is a single point in a space with that many dimensions.
Nobody can picture 1,536 dimensions, and nobody needs to. The only thing that
matters is a measure of how close two points are.

The usual measure is cosine similarity. It compares the direction two vectors
point, ignoring their length, and returns a number from 1 down to -1. A score of
1 means the two texts point the same way, meaning nearly the same thing. A score
near 0 means unrelated. Negative means opposite. Most retrieval systems rank
passages by cosine similarity to the question and take the highest scorers.

The whole mechanic fits in a few lines of Python. Real embeddings would come from
a model, but shrinking each text to three hand-picked numbers keeps the math
visible.

```python
import math

def cosine(a, b):
    dot = sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))
    mag_a = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in a))
    mag_b = math.sqrt(sum(y * y for y in b))
    return dot / (mag_a * mag_b)

# Pretend an embedding model turned each sentence into 3 numbers.
# Real embeddings use hundreds or thousands; 3 keeps it readable.
question = [0.91, 0.10, 0.05]

chunks = {
    "Returns are accepted within 30 days of delivery.": [0.83, 0.22, 0.11],
    "The office is closed on all federal holidays.":     [0.09, 0.90, 0.16],
    "Refunds are issued to the original payment card.":  [0.62, 0.20, 0.55],
}

scored = sorted(chunks, key=lambda text: cosine(question, chunks[text]), reverse=True)
for text in scored:
    print(round(cosine(question, chunks[text]), 3), "|", text)
```

<ExpectedOutput label="ranking three passages against the question">
```
0.986 | Returns are accepted within 30 days of delivery.
0.783 | Refunds are issued to the original payment card.
0.214 | The office is closed on all federal holidays.
```
</ExpectedOutput>

The returns passage scores highest, the refund passage is a close second, and the
holidays passage sits far down where it belongs. That ranking is the entire job
of vector search: turn the question into a vector, compare it against every stored
vector, and hand back the closest. Swap in real embeddings with 1,536 numbers each
and the code is the same, only the vectors get longer and the meanings get sharper.

## The retrieval-augmented-generation pipeline

RAG has two phases. One runs once, ahead of time, to build a searchable index of
the documents. The other runs on every question. Keeping them separate is what
makes the system fast at answer time.

The build phase has three steps. First, chunk the documents. A whole document is
too big to be a good unit of retrieval, and it mixes many topics, so it gets split
into passages of a few hundred tokens each, roughly a paragraph or two. Chunks
often overlap by a sentence or so, so an idea split across a boundary still lands
whole inside one chunk. Second, embed every chunk by running it through the
embedding model, producing one vector per chunk. Third, store the vectors,
alongside the original text, in a vector store: a database built for nearest-
neighbor search, such as pgvector, FAISS, or Chroma. It indexes the vectors so a
search does not have to compare against every one by brute force.

The query phase runs when a question arrives. The question goes through the same
embedding model to become a vector. The vector store returns the top matches,
usually the closest three to eight chunks, by cosine similarity. Those chunks get
pasted into the prompt as context, above the question, with an instruction like
"answer using only the context below." That full prompt goes to the model through
the API, and the model writes an answer grounded in the retrieved text. Retrieval
finds the facts, generation writes the reply. That is the name.

In code, the query phase is short. The embedding model and the store are shown
here as functions to keep the shape clear.

```python
# Sketch: embed_text() and store are provided by an embedding
# model and a vector store, not shown here.

def answer(question, store, client):
    q_vector = embed_text(question)
    top_chunks = store.search(q_vector, k=4)   # 4 closest passages

    context = "\n\n".join(top_chunks)
    prompt = (
        "Answer using only the context below. "
        "If the answer is not in it, say you do not know.\n\n"
        f"Context:\n{context}\n\n"
        f"Question: {question}"
    )
    return client.generate(prompt)
```

Trace the support bot through it. The handbook gets chopped into 400 chunks, each
embedded and stored, once. A user asks, "Can I return shoes I bought three weeks
ago?" That question becomes a vector, the store returns the four nearest chunks,
and the top one is "Returns are accepted within 30 days of delivery." The prompt
now carries the real policy, so the model answers, "Yes, the window is 30 days
and three weeks is inside it," and it can point at the passage it used. The model
never memorized the policy. It read it at answer time.

<Callout type="warning" title="Retrieval quality is the ceiling">
RAG does not make a model honest. If the search returns the wrong chunks, or the
answer is in no chunk at all, the model can still invent one. Grounding is only as
good as retrieval, which is why the instruction above tells the model to say it
does not know when the context does not cover the question. That one sentence
turns many confident wrong answers into an honest "not in the documents."
</Callout>

<Exercise title="Chunk a document you know">
Pick a document you know well: a class syllabus, a game's rulebook, a product's
FAQ. Split it in your head into passages of a paragraph or two, the way the build
phase would. Count how many chunks you get.

Now write down a question a real person would ask it, phrased in everyday words
that do not match the document's headings. Which one chunk holds the answer? That
chunk is what vector search has to surface, and your question is what gets
embedded and compared against every chunk you just counted. If two chunks both
look relevant, that is the case where retrieving the top three or four, instead of
just one, saves the answer.
</Exercise>

RAG gives a model the right facts, but the model still only writes an answer. It
does not check the order status, book the return, or run a search when the first
retrieval comes up empty. The next lesson, ai-agents-and-tools, is about a model
that does not just answer from what it is handed but decides what to do next and
takes the action itself.
