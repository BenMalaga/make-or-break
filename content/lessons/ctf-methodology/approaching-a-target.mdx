A CTF box is not a riddle you crack by inspiration. It is a system, and systems
give way to process. The players who root machines consistently are not smarter
than you. They are more methodical. Every target, from an easy warmup to a hard
insane-rated box, yields to the same loop.

## The loop

Four phases, always in this order:

1. **Enumerate.** Map everything the target exposes: open ports, service
   versions, web pages, usernames, readable files. You cannot attack what you
   have not found.
2. **Foothold.** Turn one finding into code execution. A vulnerable service, a
   default credential, an unrestricted upload. The goal is a shell as any user,
   however weak.
3. **Escalate.** Go from that first low-privilege user to root or SYSTEM.
   Re-enumerate, this time from the inside.
4. **Flag.** Collect the proof. Usually `user.txt` in a home directory and
   `root.txt` in `/root`.

The trap beginners fall into is jumping straight to phase 2, firing exploits at
the first open port they see. Discipline is doing phase 1 completely before you
touch phase 2.

## Enumeration is the whole game

Start every box the same way: a full picture of what is listening. A fast port
sweep tells you what is open, then a targeted version scan tells you what it is.

## Take notes like it is evidence

The single habit that separates people who finish boxes from people who spin out
is note-taking. Give every target its own folder before you scan. Log the IP,
every open port and version, every credential you find, and every command that
actually worked. When you land a shell three hours in and need that password you
saw in phase 1, it will be one grep away instead of gone.

<CommandBlock
  title="attacker@kali"
  commands={[
    { cmd: "mkdir -p ~/boxes/target/{scans,loot,exploits}", comment: "one home per box" },
    { cmd: "nmap -sC -sV -p- -oA ~/boxes/target/scans/full $IP", comment: "-oA saves all three output formats" },
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="security" title="Stuck means under-enumerated">
When you hit a wall, resist the urge to hunt for a fancier exploit. Nine times
out of ten you missed something: a virtual host, a second port, a comment in
page source, a subdirectory. Go back to phase 1 and look harder.
</Callout>
