In your-first-circuit, a battery lit an LED. Wire a battery to a resistor to an
LED and back into a loop, and the light comes on and stays on. It is a
satisfying first result, and it has one flaw: the battery does exactly one
thing, forever. It cannot make the light blink. It cannot wait two seconds and
then switch off. It cannot notice a button being pressed and light up only then.
A battery pushes current and holds no opinions.

Making a light do something in time, or in response to the world, needs a part
that can decide: on now, off in a second, on again when something happens. That
part is a microcontroller, and getting one to blink a single LED is the doorway
into every board, robot, and gadget that follows.

## The computer on a single chip

A microcontroller is a complete, small computer built onto one chip of silicon.
Not a computer wired to a screen and a keyboard: a bare one, about the size of a
fingernail, meant to be soldered onto a board and left to run one program.
Everything a computer needs sits inside that chip. A processor that carries out
instructions. Memory that holds the program and the handful of numbers it works
with while running. And the part that sets it apart from a laptop, a row of pins
it can control directly.

A pin is one metal leg on the edge of the chip, a single electrical connection to
the outside world. The chip can read the voltage on a pin, or set the voltage on
a pin, under the command of the program running inside it. That is the whole
trick. A laptop processor does its work behind glass and never touches a wire on
purpose. A microcontroller exists to touch wires: to switch things on, and to
sense whether something out there is on or off.

Real numbers make it concrete. The chip at the center of the most common beginner
board, the ATmega328P, runs at 16 MHz, meaning its processor steps through 16
million basic operations a second. It holds 32 KB of flash memory for the program
(flash keeps its contents with the power off, so the program survives being
unplugged) and 2 KB of SRAM for the values the program juggles while it runs. It
controls 23 pins. A modern laptop has billions of times more memory and a
processor hundreds of times faster. None of that helps blink an LED. The small
chip is not a weak laptop. It is a different tool, aimed at controlling physical
things cheaply and reliably, and it costs a couple of dollars.

## Arduino is the board and the on-ramp

A bare ATmega328P is awkward to use on its own. It needs a steady power supply, a
clock to pace it, and a special piece of hardware to load a program onto it.
Arduino removes that friction. Arduino is two things wearing one name: a family of
circuit boards that wrap a microcontroller in the supporting parts it needs, and
a plain system for writing programs and loading them onto that chip over an
ordinary USB cable.

The board most people start with is the Arduino Uno. It carries the ATmega328P, a
USB socket, a power regulator, and two rows of labeled sockets called headers that
bring the chip's pins out where a wire can reach them. The Uno labels 14 of those
pins for digital use, numbered 0 to 13, plus 6 more for reading analog signals, a
split a later lesson picks up. A wire pushed into the socket marked 13 connects to
pin 13 of the chip.

A program written for an Arduino is called a sketch. The language is close to C
and C++, but the ideas carry across every language: prior lessons described code
as a list of exact steps a machine follows in order, and a sketch is exactly that,
with one wrinkle specific to hardware that the code below makes clear.

## Digital output: two voltages, nothing between

The simplest thing a microcontroller can do to the world is set one pin to one of
two voltages. That is called digital output. Digital here means two states and no
middle ground: fully on or fully off, like a wall switch, not a dimmer.

On an Arduino Uno the two states have names and real values. HIGH means the pin is
connected, inside the chip, to the board's 5 volt supply. (Voltage is the
electrical push that drives current through a wire, measured in volts.) LOW means
the pin is connected to 0 volts, the board's ground, its electrical zero. A pin
set HIGH can push current out through a wire. A pin set LOW cannot; it sits at
zero.

Two instructions cover it. pinMode tells the chip that a given pin will be used
for output rather than input, which has to be said once before the pin is driven.
digitalWrite then sets that pin HIGH or LOW. None of this is a metaphor.
`digitalWrite(13, HIGH)` physically raises pin 13 to 5 volts, and it stays there
until something sets it LOW again.

## The pin takes the battery's place

Here is where your-first-circuit comes back. That lesson built a loop: a battery,
a resistor, and an LED, wired on a breadboard, the plastic board with rows of
clip-in holes that joins parts without soldering. Current left the battery's
positive side, flowed through the resistor, through the LED, and back to the
battery's negative side. The LED lit. The resistor was mandatory: an LED cannot
limit its own current, and left alone across a battery it pulls until it burns
out. The resistor holds the current down to a safe level.

Swapping in a microcontroller changes exactly one part of that picture. The
Arduino pin takes the place of the battery's positive terminal. When the program
sets pin 13 HIGH, the pin sits at 5 volts, current flows out of it, through the
resistor, through the LED, and into one of the board's ground pins, which plays
the role the battery's negative terminal used to. Set the pin LOW and it drops to
0 volts, current stops, and the LED goes dark. The program, not a hand moving a
wire, now opens and closes that loop.

Everything else is the same, and that includes the resistor. The LED still cannot
limit its own current, so the resistor is still doing the same job, now against 5
volts from a pin instead of the voltage from a battery. Skip it and the LED tries
to pull far more current than the pin can safely give, which damages the LED, the
pin, or both. (Current is the flow of charge through the circuit, measured in
amps, or in thousandths of an amp, milliamps.)

The value comes from Ohm's law, from the earlier electrical basics: current
equals voltage divided by resistance, so resistance equals voltage divided by
current. The pin supplies 5 volts. A standard red LED drops about 2 volts across
itself when lit, which leaves 5 minus 2, or 3 volts, for the resistor to work
against. A safe, bright current for that LED is about 10 milliamps, well under the
20 milliamps an Uno pin is rated to supply comfortably (40 milliamps is the
absolute limit before the pin is damaged). So:

```text
R = (5 V - 2 V) / 0.010 A = 3 V / 0.010 A = 300 ohms
```

No resistor is sold as exactly 300 ohms, so the nearest common value up is 330
ohms. At 330 ohms the current lands at 3 V divided by 330 ohms, about 9
milliamps: bright, and safely inside the pin's limit. A 220 ohm resistor works
too, at about 14 milliamps, a little brighter and still safe. That one resistor is
the entire difference between a circuit that runs for years and one that kills a
pin the first time the program sets it HIGH.

## The blink program

The classic first Arduino program blinks an LED on and off, one second each way.
It uses every idea so far. Every Arduino sketch is built from two named blocks,
and this one shows both. A named, reusable group of instructions is a function,
from prior lessons. `setup()` is the function that runs one time the instant the
board powers on or resets. `loop()` is the function that runs right after and then
repeats from its first line forever, because a microcontroller has no desktop to
return to. That endless repeat is the normal life of the chip.

Here is the whole sketch:

```cpp
// Blink: one second on, one second off, forever.

const int LED_PIN = 13;

void setup() {
  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT);
}

void loop() {
  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, HIGH);  // pin 13 to 5 V: current flows, LED on
  delay(1000);                  // hold for 1000 ms (one second)
  digitalWrite(LED_PIN, LOW);   // pin 13 to 0 V: current stops, LED off
  delay(1000);                  // hold for one more second
}
```

Every line earns its place. The first real line, `const int LED_PIN = 13`, gives
the number 13 a name. `const` means the value never changes; `int` means it is a
whole number. Naming the pin once means it can be moved to a different socket by
editing one line instead of hunting through the code.

`setup()` holds a single instruction: `pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT)`, which tells the
chip that pin 13 will drive current out rather than read it in. This runs once at
power-up. Declaring a pin's direction before using it is required; a pin left in
its default input state cannot reliably drive an LED.

`loop()` holds four instructions and runs them top to bottom, then repeats from
the top for as long as the board has power. `digitalWrite(LED_PIN, HIGH)` raises
pin 13 to 5 volts, current flows through the resistor and LED, and the light comes
on. `delay(1000)` pauses the program for 1000 milliseconds, one full second (a
millisecond is a thousandth of a second), holding the pin HIGH the whole time.
`digitalWrite(LED_PIN, LOW)` drops the pin to 0 volts and the LED goes dark. The
last `delay(1000)` holds that dark second, then `loop()` returns to its first line
and the cycle begins again. On, one second. Off, one second. Forever.

<Callout type="tip" title="Pin 13 blinks even with nothing wired">
The Uno wires pin 13 to a small LED soldered onto the board, with its own resistor
already in place. That means this exact sketch blinks a light on the board itself,
before any external LED, resistor, or breadboard is connected. It is the fastest
way to confirm a fresh board is alive and running the program that was loaded.
</Callout>

## Compiling and uploading

The sketch is text. The ATmega328P does not read C++. Something has to translate
the sketch into the raw numeric instructions the chip runs, then copy those
instructions into its flash memory. Two steps: compile, then upload.

The Arduino IDE does both behind one button, and the command line shows the same
steps plainly. The Arduino CLI tool compiles a sketch with one command.

<CommandCard
  command="arduino-cli compile --fqbn arduino:avr:uno Blink"
  title="terminal"
  explain="Translates the sketch in the folder named Blink into instructions for the Uno. The --fqbn flag names the exact board so the compiler targets the right chip; get it wrong and the code will not run."
/>

<ExpectedOutput label="compiling the Blink sketch">
```
Sketch uses 924 bytes (2%) of program storage space. Maximum is 32256 bytes.
Global variables use 9 bytes (0%) of dynamic memory, leaving 2039 bytes for local variables. Maximum is 2048 bytes.
```
</ExpectedOutput>

Those two lines are worth reading, not skipping. The whole Blink program is 924
bytes, using 2 percent of the chip's 32256 bytes of program storage (32 KB of
flash, minus 512 bytes the board reserves for its own start-up code). That leaves
enormous room, which is the point: a microcontroller program is small on purpose.
The exact byte counts shift a little with toolchain versions, but the shape holds.

Compiling only checks and translates. Uploading copies the result onto the chip:
`arduino-cli upload -p /dev/ttyACM0 --fqbn arduino:avr:uno Blink` sends the
compiled program down the USB cable and writes it into flash. The port name,
`/dev/ttyACM0` here, is where the board appears to the computer and varies by
system. A second later the LED starts blinking, and it keeps blinking with the USB
cable unplugged and a battery attached, because flash held the program through the
power cycle.

<Exercise title="Trace the current, then change the rhythm">
Before touching any hardware, follow the current in the Blink circuit on paper.
Start at pin 13 the moment the program has set it HIGH. Name every part the
current passes through, in order, until it reaches ground. Write down where the
resistor sits, then say in one sentence what would happen to the LED and the pin
if it were removed.

Now change the timing without rewiring anything. In the sketch, set the first
delay to 100 and leave the second at 1000. Predict what the light does before
running it: a short flash, then a long dark gap, repeating. If an Arduino and an
LED are within reach, wire pin 13 through a 330 ohm resistor to the LED's long
leg, the LED's short leg to a ground pin, upload the sketch, and check your
prediction against the board.

If the light stays dark, do not rewrite the program. Check three things in order:
the LED's legs may be reversed (it passes current only one way), the resistor may
sit in the wrong row of the breadboard, or the wrong pin number may be in the
code. Each is a five second fix once found.
</Exercise>

Blink proves a microcontroller can drive the world on a schedule of its own. It is
still one direction of traffic: the chip talks, the LED listens, and nothing comes
back. A pin can also run the other way, reading whether the voltage on it is high
or low, which is how a board notices a button press, a flipped switch, or a sensor
reporting the temperature. The next lesson, inputs-outputs-and-sensors, turns the
pins around and lets the board read the world instead of only lighting it.
