The blink sketch from the last lesson has a flaw nobody points out: the LED does
the exact same thing forever. Leave the board in a drawer for a year, power it
back up, and it blinks at the identical rate, because nothing outside the board
can change its mind. A microcontroller that only sends signals out is a fancy
timer.

The interesting part starts when the board reads something first. A button. A
knob. The brightness of the room. Then it decides what to do based on what it
found, and acts. Read, decide, act. That loop is the whole personality of every
gadget that reacts to the world, and this lesson builds it one piece at a time,
using the same `setup` and `loop` structure the Arduino lesson set up.

## Reading a pin as a switch

The last lesson used `digitalWrite` to push a pin HIGH or LOW, sending 5 volts
or 0 volts out to an LED. Reading works the other direction. `digitalRead` looks
at a pin and reports the voltage it finds there, rounded to one of two answers:
HIGH if the voltage is near 5 volts, LOW if it is near 0.

Before a pin can be read it has to be told it is an input. In `setup`,
`pinMode(2, INPUT)` sets pin 2, one of the numbered connection points along the
board's edge, to listen instead of drive. After that, `digitalRead(2)` returns
HIGH or LOW every time it is called.

A pushbutton is the simplest thing to read. Inside it, two metal contacts sit
apart until a press pushes them together. Wire one side of the button to 5 volts
and the other side to pin 2, and pressing the button connects 5 volts straight
to the pin, so `digitalRead` sees HIGH. Let go, and the contacts spring apart
again.

Here is the trap. When the button is released, pin 2 is connected to nothing at
all.

## The floating pin, and the resistor that fixes it

An input pin connected to nothing does not read 0. It floats. A floating pin
acts like a tiny antenna: it picks up electrical noise from the air, from nearby
wires, from the board's own power, and its reading flickers between HIGH and LOW
at random. A button wired with one loose end works maybe half the time and looks
haunted the rest.

The fix is a resistor that gives the pin a definite voltage to rest at whenever
the button is doing nothing. There are two ways to place it.

A pull-down resistor connects the pin to ground, 0 volts, through a resistor,
usually 10 kilohms. With the button released, the pin sees 0 volts through that
resistor and reads LOW, steadily. Press the button and 5 volts arrives from the
other side. The direct path to 5 volts is far easier than the path through
10 kilohms to ground, so the pin jumps to HIGH. Release it and the pin drains
back to LOW. The resistor is deliberately large. When the button is pressed, the
only current it wastes is 5 volts across 10 kilohms, which Ohm's law puts at
5 / 10000, or 0.5 milliamps. Small enough to ignore, large enough to hold the
pin still.

A pull-up resistor is the mirror image. It ties the pin to 5 volts through the
resistor, so the idle pin reads HIGH, and the button connects the pin to ground
when pressed, pulling it LOW. The logic is inverted, pressed means LOW, which
trips people up until they trace the wiring once.

Arduino boards ship with pull-up resistors built into the chip, which saves a
part and a hole on the breadboard. `pinMode(2, INPUT_PULLUP)` switches on an
internal pull-up somewhere between 20 and 50 kilohms, so the pin idles HIGH and
a button wired from the pin to ground reads LOW when pressed. Most real projects
reach for `INPUT_PULLUP` for exactly this reason.

```cpp
const int BUTTON_PIN = 2;
const int LED_PIN = 13;   // the Uno's built-in LED

void setup() {
  pinMode(BUTTON_PIN, INPUT_PULLUP);  // idle reads HIGH
  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT);
}

void loop() {
  if (digitalRead(BUTTON_PIN) == LOW) {   // pressed pulls the pin to ground
    digitalWrite(LED_PIN, HIGH);
  } else {
    digitalWrite(LED_PIN, LOW);
  }
}
```

Pin 13 earns a mention: on an Arduino Uno it is wired to a small LED soldered
onto the board itself, so that sketch lights the built-in LED with no extra
parts at all.

## Reading a voltage, not just on or off

A button has two states. A knob has a thousand. `digitalRead` cannot tell a
quarter turn from a half turn, because it flattens everything down to HIGH or
LOW. Reading a position, a temperature, or a brightness needs a pin that reports
how much voltage it sees, not just whether there is a lot or a little.

That is analog input, and it runs through a piece of the chip called the
analog-to-digital converter, or ADC. The ADC measures the voltage on a pin and
turns it into a number. On the Uno the ADC is 10-bit, meaning it sorts the
voltage into 2 to the 10th power, 1024, evenly spaced steps. The reading comes
back as a whole number from 0 to 1023. Zero volts reads 0. Five volts reads
1023. Halfway, 2.5 volts, reads about 512. Each step covers 5 volts divided by
1024, roughly 4.9 millivolts. The `analogRead(A0)` function does the measuring,
and only the pins labeled A0 through A5 can do it.

A potentiometer is the classic thing to read this way. It is a resistor with a
slider: three pins, the two ends and a wiper that taps off somewhere in the
middle. Wire the two ends across 5 volts and ground and the wiper becomes a
voltage divider, a pair of resistances that splits the supply and hands the
middle pin any voltage from 0 to 5 depending on how far the knob is turned. Feed
the wiper into A0 and `analogRead` reports the knob's position as a number.

```cpp
int position = analogRead(A0);   // 0 turned one way, 1023 turned the other
```

A light sensor pulls the same trick with a different part. A photoresistor is a
component whose resistance falls as light hits it. Put it in a voltage divider
with an ordinary fixed resistor and the divider's middle voltage now tracks the
light. Wire the photoresistor between 5 volts and the sensor pin, and a
10 kilohm resistor from that pin to ground. Bright light drops the
photoresistor's resistance and pushes the pin toward 5 volts, so `analogRead`
climbs. Darkness raises its resistance and sends the number back down. The board
cannot see, but it can read a value that rises and falls with the room.

## Outputs past the LED

An LED is a gentle load. It asks for maybe 10 to 15 milliamps, and a pin hands
that over without complaint. Not every output is so polite.

A piezo buzzer makes sound, and a passive one needs a signal that swings on and
off thousands of times a second to vibrate its element. `digitalWrite` is too
slow to do that by hand, so Arduino provides `tone`. `tone(8, 1000)` drives pin
8 with a 1000 hertz square wave, a 1000 hertz note, until `noTone(8)` stops it.
A buzzer wired between pin 8 and ground plays whatever frequency `tone` is
handed.

```cpp
tone(8, 1000);   // 1000 Hz on pin 8
delay(200);
noTone(8);       // silence
```

A motor is where a bare pin gives out. A small hobby DC motor pulls a few
hundred milliamps when it spins freely, and far more the instant it starts or
stalls. An Uno's output pin is rated to supply about 20 milliamps safely, and
40 milliamps is the chip's absolute limit. Wire a motor straight from a pin to
ground and the pin simply cannot deliver that much current. At best the motor
barely twitches. At worst the pin burns out, or the voltage across the whole
board sags far enough that the microcontroller resets mid-spin.

The answer is to stop asking the pin to carry the power at all. A transistor is
a switch controlled by a tiny current: a small signal on one leg lets a much
larger current flow through the other two. The pin drives the transistor with
its harmless 20 milliamps, and the transistor switches the motor's heavy current
from a separate power supply. A motor driver is a chip with those transistors
already inside, plus the protection they need, so one part can run a motor or
two. One piece of that protection is worth naming: a motor is a coil, and a coil
fights any sudden change in its current by throwing a sharp voltage spike the
moment it switches off. A flyback diode placed across the motor gives that spike
a safe path to burn off, instead of letting it punch back into the transistor.
Direct-drive an LED. Never direct-drive a motor.

## Read, decide, act: a nightlight

Put the three moves together into one small machine. Read the light in the room,
decide whether it counts as dark, and switch an LED on when it does. A
nightlight, in about a dozen lines.

```cpp
const int SENSOR_PIN = A0;    // photoresistor divider feeds this pin
const int LED_PIN = 9;        // LED to ground through a 220 ohm resistor
const int DARK_LEVEL = 400;   // readings below this count as dark

void setup() {
  pinMode(LED_PIN, OUTPUT);
  Serial.begin(9600);         // open a text channel back to a computer
}

void loop() {
  int light = analogRead(SENSOR_PIN);   // 0 (dark) to 1023 (bright)
  Serial.println(light);                // print one reading per line

  if (light < DARK_LEVEL) {
    digitalWrite(LED_PIN, HIGH);        // dark room: LED on
  } else {
    digitalWrite(LED_PIN, LOW);         // enough light: LED off
  }

  delay(100);                           // ten checks a second
}
```

Every idea in this lesson is in there. `analogRead` is the reading. The `if`
comparison against `DARK_LEVEL` is the decision. `digitalWrite` is the action.
`Serial.begin(9600)` opens a text channel to a connected computer and
`Serial.println` sends one number per line, so the raw readings can be watched
while the sensor is covered and uncovered. That serial view is how a threshold
gets tuned: run it, read the numbers, pick a `DARK_LEVEL` that sits between the
lit-room value and the covered value.

The readings sit in the 600s in a lit room. When a hand covers the
photoresistor, the number dives into the 300s, below the 400 threshold, so the
LED comes on. Lift the hand and the value climbs back over 400 and the LED goes
dark again. Nothing in the sketch knows what a hand is. It only compares one
number to another, ten times a second, and acts on the result.

<Callout type="tip" title="Tune the threshold to the actual room">
A DARK_LEVEL of 400 is a starting guess, not a law. A photoresistor's readings
depend on the fixed resistor paired with it, the supply voltage, and how bright
the room is to begin with. Watch the serial monitor in the real space, note the
number with the sensor lit and the number with it covered, and set the threshold
between them. Copying a value from a tutorial and skipping this step is the most
common reason a sensor project half-works.
</Callout>

<Exercise title="Build the nightlight, or trace it">
If an Arduino and a photoresistor are in reach, build the divider: photoresistor
from 5V to A0, a 10 kilohm resistor from A0 to ground, and an LED with its 220
ohm resistor from pin 9 to ground. Upload the nightlight sketch and open the
serial monitor. Watch the number in a normal room, then cup your hand over the
sensor and watch it fall. Set DARK_LEVEL a little above the covered reading and
below the lit reading, upload again, and the LED should snap on the moment you
shade the sensor.

No hardware handy? Read the loop and answer one question. If the photoresistor
and the fixed resistor swapped places in the divider, would covering the sensor
make the number go up or down, and which way would the comparison have to change
so the LED still turns on in the dark? Working that out on paper is the same
skill as debugging it on the bench.
</Exercise>

Every circuit in this module has lived on a breadboard, with parts pushed into
holes and jumper wires bridging them. That is how a design gets tried, not how it
ships. A breadboard joint is loose, it wanders, and it falls apart the first time
the board is bumped. Turning a working breadboard into a solid, repeatable object
means drawing these same connections as copper traces on a printed circuit board,
a PCB, and having it manufactured. That is where the hardware track goes next:
PCB design, where the exact circuits from this module become a board that can be
held.
