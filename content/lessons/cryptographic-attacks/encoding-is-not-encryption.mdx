You pull a session cookie off an app and it looks like this: `eyJ1c2VyIjoiYWRtaW4ifQ==`. Random gibberish, right? Paste it into a base64 decoder and it says `{"user":"admin"}`. That took two seconds and no key. Whoever built that app confused encoding with encryption, and it is one of the most common mistakes in web security.

Here is the distinction that matters. Encoding is a reversible format change with no secret. Anyone can reverse it. Encryption is a reversible transformation that requires a key, so only a key holder can reverse it. Hashing is one-way and reversible by no one. Three different jobs. People blur them constantly, usually because base64 output happens to look scrambled to a human eye.

## Reading the shapes

You want to recognize encodings without decoding first, because it tells you what tool to reach for.

Base64 uses `A-Z`, `a-z`, `0-9`, `+`, and `/`, often ending in one or two `=` padding characters. Length is always a multiple of four. That trailing `==` is the loudest tell there is.

Hex uses only `0-9` and `a-f`, and its length is always even. A 32-character hex string is 16 bytes, which is a hint it might be an MD5 digest or a 128-bit value. URL encoding is the one full of `%` signs, like `%2F` for a slash.

## Why this is a real vulnerability

The failure is not the encoding itself. Encoding is fine and necessary, it moves binary safely through text channels like HTTP headers, JSON, and URLs. The failure is trusting it to hide or protect anything.

When an app stores `role=YWRtaW4=` in a cookie and reads it back without a signature, you decode it, change `user` to `admin`, re-encode, and send it back. Nothing stops you, because there was never a secret involved. This is exactly how unsigned session tokens and tampered JWTs get abused. A JWT with `"alg":"none"` is the same trap: the payload is just base64, and if the server does not verify the signature, you rewrite the payload freely.

<Callout type="security" title="Decode only what you are cleared to touch">
Decoding a token you captured from your own test account in an authorized assessment is fair game. Grabbing another user's cookie, or tampering with tokens on a production app you have no scope for, crosses into unauthorized access. Keep it to your own accounts and in-scope targets.
</Callout>

The habit to build: whenever a value looks scrambled, try to decode it before you assume it is protected. If it decodes with no key, it was never protected. Real protection leaves a signature or an authentication tag you cannot forge, and it does not fall over to a two-second `base64 -d`.
