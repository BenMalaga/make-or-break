Dump a user table and find `5f4dcc3b5aa765d61d8327deb882cf99` in the password column. You do not crack that. You paste it into a search engine and get back `password` in the first result, because that MD5 digest has been in public lookup tables for years. Unsalted fast hashes are not a wall, they are a speed bump, and this lesson is about why.

Three properties decide how hard a stored hash is to attack: whether it is salted, how fast the function is, and whether it is broken outright. Get all three wrong and the hash may as well be plaintext.

## Rainbow tables and why salt breaks them

A rainbow table is a giant precomputed mapping from candidate passwords to their hashes. Someone hashed millions of common passwords ahead of time. If the app stores a raw `MD5(password)` or `SHA1(password)`, cracking is just a lookup, and the work was done years ago by someone else.

A salt kills that dead. A salt is a unique random value mixed into each password before hashing, so two users who both chose `Summer2024` get completely different stored digests. Now the attacker cannot use a shared precomputed table, because the table would have to be rebuilt for every salt. They are forced to attack one hash at a time.

That is why salt matters more than most people think. It does not make any single password harder to guess. It removes the shortcut of attacking everyone at once.

## Fast versus slow functions

MD5, SHA-1, and SHA-256 are fast by design, which is great for file integrity and terrible for passwords. A modern GPU rig runs billions of MD5 guesses per second. bcrypt, scrypt, and Argon2 are deliberately slow and memory-hungry, so the same rig manages maybe tens of thousands of guesses per second. That gap of six or seven orders of magnitude is the entire point of a password hashing function.

Identify before you attack. A 32-character hex string is probably MD5 or NTLM. A `$2b$` prefix is bcrypt and may not be worth your GPU time at all.

## Length extension, the concept

This one trips people up, so hold the idea, not the math. Functions built on the Merkle-Damgard construction, which includes MD5, SHA-1, and SHA-256, hash data in blocks and carry forward an internal state. The final digest is that internal state. That is the flaw.

If an app authenticates a message with `hash(secret + message)`, an attacker who knows the digest and the message length can resume the hash from that state and append their own data, producing a valid digest for `secret + message + attacker_data` without ever knowing the secret. They extend the message and forge a new valid tag. The tool `hashpump` does the arithmetic for you.

The fix is HMAC. HMAC nests the key in a way that does not expose the raw internal state, so the extension trick has nothing to resume from. When you see a homemade `hash(secret + data)` authenticator, flag it and reach for the extension attack.

<Callout type="security" title="Cracked hashes are still stolen credentials">
Cracking hashes from a data set you were authorized to test, or from a public wordlist challenge, is normal practice. Cracking a real leak of other people's passwords, or using recovered credentials outside your engagement scope, is illegal and unethical. Recovered passwords stay in the report, not in a login form on some other site.
</Callout>
