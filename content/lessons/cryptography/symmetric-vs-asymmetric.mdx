Two strangers want to trade secrets over a wire that anyone can tap. That is the
whole problem cryptography was built to solve. A shopper sends a card number to a
store. A reporter messages a source. In every case the message crosses routers,
cables, and radios that other people own, and any one of them can copy every byte
in transit. Secrecy has to survive an audience that reads the entire conversation.

Encryption is the tool for that. It takes readable data, the plaintext, and
scrambles it into ciphertext that looks like noise. A key, a secret value,
controls the scrambling. Whoever holds the right key can reverse the process and
recover the plaintext. Anyone without it sees garbage. The card number becomes an
unreadable block on the wire, and only the intended store can turn it back into
digits.

One rule sits under all of this, and getting it wrong has sunk real systems. The
secret is the key, never the algorithm. This is Kerckhoffs's principle, stated in
the 1880s and proven right since: a cipher should stay secure even when everyone
knows exactly how it works, as long as the key stays hidden. The math behind AES
is published, taught in classes, and shipped in open source. Its security rests
entirely on the key. A design that depends on keeping the algorithm itself secret
is called security through obscurity, and it collapses the moment someone
reverse-engineers the code, which someone always does.

The reasoning is plain. A public algorithm gets attacked by thousands of
researchers for years, the weak ones break in the open, and the survivors earn
trust. A secret homegrown cipher gets reviewed by nobody until an attacker finds
the flaw quietly and keeps it. Openness is a feature here, not a risk.

## Symmetric encryption: one shared key

The oldest shape of encryption uses a single key for both directions. The same
secret both encrypts the plaintext and decrypts the ciphertext. This is symmetric
encryption. Both parties hold identical copies of the key, they agree on the
cipher, and traffic flows.

AES, the Advanced Encryption Standard, is the symmetric cipher almost everything
reaches for. It is fast, it runs in dedicated CPU instructions on modern chips,
and at a 128-bit or 256-bit key it has no practical break. A phone can encrypt a
video call with AES without the battery noticing. That speed is the reason
symmetric crypto carries nearly all of the bulk data moving across the internet.

Key length is what makes AES hard to guess by force. A 128-bit key has about
three hundred and forty undecillion possible values, a 39-digit number, and an
attacker with no shortcut has to try them. No amount of current hardware brings a
brute-force sweep of that space into reach, so a well-chosen AES key does not fall
to guessing. The weakness, when one exists, lives somewhere other than the key
length.

Then comes the catch that symmetric encryption cannot solve on its own. Both sides
need the same key before they can talk, and they are strangers on an open wire.
Handing the key over means sending it across the exact channel the eavesdropper is
watching. Send the AES key in the clear and the attacker copies it, then decrypts
everything that follows. This is the key-distribution problem, sometimes called
key exchange. For most of history the only answer was to meet in person or trust a
courier with the key. Two people who have never met, connecting for the first time
over the public internet, have no courier. That gap is what the next idea fills.

## Asymmetric encryption: a key that comes in a pair

Public-key cryptography breaks the assumption that the encrypting key and the
decrypting key have to be the same value. Instead each party generates a keypair:
two mathematically linked keys where one undoes what the other does. One is the
public key, published to the whole world. The other is the private key, kept
secret and never shared. Data scrambled with the public key can be unscrambled
only with the matching private key.

Here is what that buys. A store publishes its public key openly. Anyone, including
a first-time visitor, can encrypt a message with that public key, and only the
store, holding the private key, can read it. The eavesdropper on the wire sees the
public key and the ciphertext and still cannot decrypt, because the public key does
not reverse its own work. The secret never had to cross the channel at all. That is
the key-distribution problem solved.

Two families of asymmetric algorithms carry most of this load. RSA rests on the
difficulty of factoring the product of two very large prime numbers: multiplying
the primes is easy, pulling the product back apart is not, and RSA keys run 2048 or
4096 bits to keep that factoring out of reach. Elliptic-curve cryptography, ECC,
rests on a different hard problem defined over points on a curve, and it reaches the
same strength with far smaller keys. A 256-bit elliptic-curve key stands in for a
3072-bit RSA key, so it computes quicker and stores lighter, which is why newer
systems tend to pick it.

The same keypair does two separate jobs, and mixing them up causes real confusion
later. In the direction covered so far, others encrypt to the store with its
public key so that only the private key can read the result. Run the pair the
other way and it proves who sent a message: the store signs with its private key,
and anyone can check that signature with the published public key, since only the
holder of the private key could have produced it. Encryption keeps a message
secret. Signing proves who wrote it. Both fall out of one pair of keys, and a
later lesson leans on the signing direction hard.

There is a cost, and it is the reason asymmetric crypto is not the whole answer.
The math is slow. Encrypting a large file directly with RSA or ECC would crawl,
often hundreds of times slower than AES on the same bytes. Public-key operations
are fine for a handful of small messages and painful for a video stream.

## The hybrid design that runs the real internet

Neither cipher is enough alone. Symmetric is fast but cannot get its key across an
open wire. Asymmetric gets a secret across the wire but is too slow for bulk data.
The fix is to use each for the job it is good at, and that pairing is called hybrid
encryption. It is the default design of TLS, the protocol behind every https
address, and of secure messaging apps.

A concrete connection runs like this. A browser opens a session with a shop's
server.

1. The server sends its certificate, which contains its public key. A later lesson
   covers how the browser confirms that certificate is genuine and not an impostor's.
2. The two sides run a public-key exchange. In the classic version the browser
   generates a fresh random value, encrypts it with the server's public key, and
   sends it over; only the server's private key can recover it. Both sides now hold
   the same random secret, and nobody watching the wire has it.
3. From that shared secret, both derive the same symmetric session key, a throwaway
   AES key good for this one connection.
4. The slow asymmetric step is finished. Every byte of real traffic after that, the
   pages, the card number, the messages, rides on fast AES under the session key.

Two phases, each playing to its strength. Asymmetric crypto solves distribution
once, at the start of the connection. Symmetric crypto carries the load for the rest
of the session. Modern TLS refines step two with an ephemeral exchange, ECDHE, so
that even a later-stolen private key cannot decrypt recordings of past sessions, a
property called forward secrecy. The two-phase shape stays the same: agree on a
symmetric key using asymmetric math, then switch to symmetric for speed.

<Callout type="security" title="Where attackers actually go">
Because the bulk data rides on AES, and AES has no practical break, an attacker
rarely bothers with the cipher itself. The soft targets are the key steps around
it: a weak random number generator that makes the session key guessable, a private
key left readable on a stolen backup, a forced downgrade to an old broken exchange.
Strong ciphers with sloppy key handling fail constantly. The keys are the target,
not the algorithm.
</Callout>

<Exercise title="Spot both ciphers in one real handshake">
On a machine with openssl installed, run `openssl s_client -connect wikipedia.org:443 </dev/null 2>/dev/null | openssl x509 -noout -text` and find the `Public Key Algorithm` line in the certificate. It will read something like `rsaEncryption` or `id-ecPublicKey`. That is the asymmetric key the server proves it owns.

Now run `openssl s_client -connect wikipedia.org:443 </dev/null 2>/dev/null | grep -i cipher` and read the negotiated cipher suite, a string like `TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384`. The `AES_256` in the middle is the symmetric cipher doing the real work once the handshake finishes.

No openssl handy? In a desktop browser, click the padlock in the address bar, open the certificate details, and read the public key type. Seeing an RSA or elliptic-curve key sit next to an AES cipher on the same connection is the hybrid design laid out in front of you.
</Exercise>

A quiet assumption ran through that whole handshake: the browser trusted that the
public key inside the certificate really belonged to the shop and not to an
impostor wedged in the middle. Proving that a key or a message has not been swapped
in flight is a different job from hiding it, and it starts with the one-way
fingerprint that the next lesson, Hashing and Integrity, is built on.
