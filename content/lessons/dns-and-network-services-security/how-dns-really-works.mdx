Before your browser sends a single byte to a web server, it asks a question in the dark: "what is the address for this name?" It trusts whatever answer comes back. That blind trust is the reason DNS matters to anyone doing security, and it is why understanding the plumbing pays off before you ever touch an attack.

DNS turns names people can remember into addresses machines can route. `example.com` becomes `93.184.216.34`. The whole system exists because nobody wants to memorize an IPv6 address, and because the address behind a name can change without the name changing.

## The lookup nobody watches

Say you type a hostname. Your machine's **stub resolver**, the tiny client baked into the OS, does almost no work itself. It hands the question to a **recursive resolver**, usually your ISP's box or something like `1.1.1.1` or `8.8.8.8`. That resolver does the legwork.

The recursive resolver walks the tree from the top. It asks a **root server** who handles `.com`. Root points it at the `.com` **TLD servers**. Those point it at the **authoritative** name server for `example.com`. The authoritative server is the source of truth, the one whose owner actually set the records. Its answer flows back up the chain to you.

<CommandBlock
  title="attacker@kali: watch a lookup happen"

    { cmd: "dig example.com A", comment: "the everyday query: give me the A record" },
    { cmd: "dig +trace example.com", comment: "walk root to TLD to authoritative, one hop at a time" },

  ]}
/>

Run `dig +trace` once and the abstract tree becomes concrete. You see the root referral, then the `.com` referral, then the final authoritative answer. That is the whole recursion, printed.

## Records that actually matter

A zone is a bag of records, each a type. You do not need all of them, but a handful come up constantly.

- **A** and **AAAA**: the IPv4 and IPv6 addresses a name points to. The payoff record.
- **CNAME**: an alias. `www` is a CNAME to the real host. Dangling CNAMEs pointing at deprovisioned cloud assets are a whole subdomain-takeover class.
- **MX**: mail servers for the domain. Read these before any phishing or email-security work.
- **TXT**: free-form text. SPF, DKIM, and domain-verification tokens live here, which makes TXT a quiet goldmine during recon.
- **NS** and **SOA**: who is authoritative, and the zone's serial and timers.

<ExpectedOutput label="dig example.com A, trimmed to what matters">
```
;; ANSWER SECTION:
example.com.  3600  IN  A  93.184.216.34

;; Query time: 24 msec
;; SERVER: 1.1.1.1#53(1.1.1.1)
```
</ExpectedOutput>

That `3600` is the **TTL**, in seconds. It tells every resolver how long it may cache this answer before asking again.

## Caching is the point, and the problem

Nobody could run the internet if every lookup walked to the root. So resolvers cache. Once `1.1.1.1` learns `example.com` is `93.184.216.34`, it holds that for the TTL and serves it to the next thousand people who ask.

Caching is why DNS is fast. It is also why DNS attacks pay off. Poison one cache entry on a busy resolver and you redirect everyone behind it until the TTL expires. Hold that thought, because it is the core of the next lesson.

<Callout type="security" title="Query only what you are cleared to enumerate">
Passive lookups against public records are low risk, but heavy enumeration, zone-transfer attempts, and brute-forced subdomain floods are active recon that some scopes forbid and some jurisdictions treat as unauthorized access. Point this at your own domains, a lab like the ones in a home range, or a target with written authorization. Stay inside the engagement scope.
</Callout>
