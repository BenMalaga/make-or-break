Exponents and Logarithms handled size: how large, how fast growing. Size alone
cannot describe a wind. A weather station reports 30 km/h from the northwest,
and those two numbers, speed and heading, do a job one number never could.
Direction is everywhere in engineering: a plane fights a crosswind, a robot arm
pushes along an axis, current flows around a loop. Direction needs geometry,
and the right triangle is the tool that turns direction into arithmetic.

## Similar triangles fix the ratios

Two triangles are similar when they have the same angles, and similar triangles
are scaled copies of each other. A triangle with sides 3, 4, and 5, and a
triangle with sides 6, 8, and 10, hold the same three angles; the second is
just the first photographed from twice as close. Every side of the big one is
exactly twice the matching side of the small one.

The consequence that powers all of trigonometry is about ratios, not lengths.
In the 3-4-5 triangle the ratio of the shortest side to the longest is 3/5 =
0.6, and in the 6-8-10 triangle it is 6/10 = 0.6, the same number. Every ratio
of sides stays fixed when a triangle is scaled, because the scaling factor
cancels between numerator and denominator. Ratios depend only on the angles.
That single fact is the foundation: once an angle is fixed, all its side ratios
are fixed with it, no matter how large or small the triangle is drawn.

This is also how heights get measured without climbing anything. A 3 meter pole
stuck in the ground casts a 4 meter shadow at some moment; at that same moment
a flagpole casts a 40 meter shadow. Sunlight strikes both at the same angle, so
the two shadow-triangles are similar, and the flagpole's height is 40 times
3/4, which is 30 meters. Thales of Miletus is credited with exactly this trick
on the Egyptian pyramids around 600 BC, and it needs nothing more than the
fixed-ratio fact above.

## Sine, cosine, and tangent

Name the parts of a right triangle relative to one chosen acute angle. The
hypotenuse is the longest side, opposite the right angle. The opposite side is
the one across from the chosen angle, and the adjacent side is the one next to
it that is not the hypotenuse. Which side is "opposite" changes when the chosen
angle changes, so the labels belong to the angle, not to the triangle.

Three ratios get names. The sine of the angle is opposite over hypotenuse. The
cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse. The tangent is opposite over adjacent. For
the 3-4-5 triangle with the angle chosen at the corner where the 3 side meets
the hypotenuse, the opposite side is 4 and the adjacent side is 3, so sin of
that angle is 4/5 = 0.8, cos is 3/5 = 0.6, and tan is 4/3, about 1.33. A few
angles come out exact and are worth knowing cold: sin of 30 degrees is exactly
0.5, cos of 60 degrees is 0.5, and tan of 45 degrees is 1, because a 45 degree
right triangle has two equal legs.

The tangent ratio connects straight back to the last lesson: for a line
climbing at an angle, the tangent of that angle is the slope, rise over run
once again. Trigonometry did not invent a new idea there; it named the slope of
a hypotenuse.

## Radians beat degrees

A degree is a historical accident: some ancient astronomer split the circle
into 360 parts, and the convention stuck. A radian is a geometric definition:
the angle that bends an arc of length equal to the radius around the edge of
the circle. Since a full circle's edge has length 2 times pi times r, a full
turn is 2 times pi radians, about 6.283. Half a turn is pi radians, so pi
radians = 180 degrees, and a quarter turn is pi/2 radians, about 1.571.
Converting is one multiplication: multiply degrees by pi/180 or radians by
180/pi.

Two things make radians the professional choice. First, arc length becomes
trivial arithmetic: an angle of theta radians sweeps an arc of length r times
theta, with no conversion constant in the way, which matters every time a wheel
rolls or a motor turns. Second, and less obvious until the next lesson, the
slope of the sine curve equals the cosine curve only when the angle is in
radians; in degrees a nuisance factor of pi/180 attaches itself to every
derivative. Working scientists write radians unless forced to do otherwise, and
Python's math module agrees: math.sin takes radians, and math.radians converts
when a degrees value arrives from elsewhere.

## The unit circle

Draw a circle of radius 1 centered where the axes cross, start measuring the
angle at due east, and sweep counterclockwise. The point where the angle's ray
meets the circle has coordinates (cos of the angle, sin of the angle): cosine
is the east-west coordinate and sine is the north-south coordinate. Every value
of sine and cosine comes from this one picture. At 0 degrees the point sits at
(1, 0), so cos is 1 and sin is 0. At 90 degrees it sits at (0, 1). Sweep past
90 degrees and the east-west coordinate goes negative, because the point has
crossed into the western half of the circle; a negative cosine is not an error,
it is the arithmetic reporting direction. This one diagram is why sine and
cosine are called circular functions, and it is the picture to summon whenever
a sign comes out unexpected.

## A vector carries size and direction

A vector is a quantity that carries a magnitude and a direction together. A
wind of 30 km/h from the northwest, a push of 50 newtons on a crate, a walk of
5 kilometers due east: all vectors. A scalar is a bare quantity with no
direction: mass, temperature, time, price. Adding scalars is ordinary
arithmetic; adding vectors needs care, because 30 km/h of wind plus 400 km/h of
plane does not produce 430 km/h unless both point the same way.

On paper a vector is an arrow whose length shows the magnitude and whose
orientation shows the direction. The picture is honest but hard to compute
with. The fix is to store the arrow as two numbers, its reach along the
east-west axis and its reach along the north-south axis, and those numbers are
called components.

## Components turn arrows into numbers

Components fall out of the triangle ratios directly. For a vector of magnitude
M pointing at an angle measured up from due east, the eastward component is M
times cos of the angle and the northward component is M times sin of the
angle. The hypotenuse is the vector itself, the adjacent side is its eastward
reach, and the opposite side is its northward reach, so cosine and sine are
just doing their original jobs.

Take the 30 km/h wind at 30 degrees north of east. The eastward component is
30 times cos 30, about 26.0 km/h. The northward component is 30 times sin 30,
exactly 15.0 km/h, because sin 30 is exactly 0.5. Every part of that arrow now
sits in ordinary numbers, and numbers add. Adding two vectors means adding
their east components together and their north components together, two
separate sums of ordinary arithmetic.

The conversion also runs backward. Given components, the magnitude comes back
out of the Pythagorean theorem, sqrt(x^2 + y^2), and the angle comes from the
tangent: the tangent of the angle above due east is the north component
divided by the east component, which is just the slope of the vector's line. A
vector of 3 east and 4 north has magnitude 5 and an angle whose tan is 4/3:
the 3-4-5 triangle wearing a compass rose. Every arrow is a triangle, and every
triangle is a pair of numbers, so nothing in this section ever needs a drawing
to be computed.

A plane heads due east at 400 km/h while that 30 km/h wind pushes it. The east
components add to 400 + 26.0 = 426.0, the north components to 0 + 15.0 = 15.0.
The plane's actual path, called the resultant, has magnitude found by the
Pythagorean theorem, sqrt(426.0^2 + 15.0^2), about 426.2 km/h, and a heading of
a bit over 2 degrees north of east. The wind nudged a 400 km/h plane by only
0.2 km/h of speed but visibly bent its heading, which is exactly the kind of
insight components make obvious and arrow-drawing hides.

```python
import math

def add(a, b):
    return [a[0] + b[0], a[1] + b[1]]

def components(speed, angle_deg):
    r = math.radians(angle_deg)
    return [speed * math.cos(r), speed * math.sin(r)]

plane = [400.0, 0.0]                  # due east, km/h
wind = components(30.0, 30)           # 30 km/h, 30 degrees north of east
total = add(plane, wind)
speed = math.hypot(total[0], total[1])
heading = math.degrees(math.atan2(total[1], total[0]))
print(round(speed, 1), round(heading, 1))
```

The print shows 426.2 and 2.0: the ground speed and the heading drift in
degrees north of east. The function components is worth keeping; the same ten
lines convert any magnitude-and-angle pair into machine-addable numbers, and
atan2 reports the resultant's angle from its components. No protractor appears
anywhere in the calculation, and that is the point: components reduce geometry
to arithmetic, and arithmetic does not need a steady hand.

<Callout type="info" title="A negative component is a direction, not a mistake">
Components go negative when a vector points west of north or south of east,
because cosine and sine turn negative past 90 and 180 degrees. The sign is the
arithmetic keeping track of direction for free: an eastward component of -8
means 8 units of reach toward the west. Fight the urge to delete minus signs;
adding vectors component by component works only if the signs stay honest.
</Callout>

Components give every quantity a size and a direction, both in plain numbers
that add. What the arithmetic still cannot answer is how any of it changes from
one moment to the next: speeds grow, headings swing, temperatures climb. Rates
and Derivatives builds the tool for change itself, and it starts by shrinking a
time interval down to nothing.
