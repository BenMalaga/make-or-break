Rates and Derivatives ended by promising the other half of calculus, and here
it is: the integral, the tool that adds up infinitely many tiny slices into one
total. A house's electricity meter performs the job physically, all day, without
knowing any calculus. Power draw through the house jumps around every second,
and the meter ignores the jumps: it multiplies each instant's watts by a sliver
of time and accumulates the products into kilowatt-hours. One kilowatt-hour is
1000 watts sustained for 3600 seconds, which is 3.6 million joules. That
running accumulation is an integral, and by the end of this lesson the meter's
arithmetic will look familiar rather than mysterious.

## Area under a curve is an accumulated total

Graph a rate, any rate: velocity against time, current against time, force
against distance. The area trapped between the curve and the horizontal axis
equals the accumulated total of that rate. The reason is slice-sized: over a
thin slice of time, velocity is nearly constant, so distance covered in that
slice is velocity times the slice's width, which is the area of a thin
rectangle. Stack the slices and the rectangles fill the region under the curve;
the sum of the rectangles is the total distance.

The constant-rate case makes the claim checkable. A cart rolling at a steady 12
m/s for 5 seconds covers 60 meters, and the graph is a rectangle 12 tall and 5
wide: 12 times 5 = 60. Area and total agree. When the rate changes, the top of
the region stops being flat and the answer stops being a one-line product, but
the meaning of the area does not change at all.

Two legs make the general case visible without any calculus. The cart rolls at
12 m/s for 5 seconds, then settles to a steady 8 m/s for 5 more. The graph is
two rectangles with areas 60 and 40, and the total distance is 100 meters.
Every integral ever computed is this same move with more rectangles: chop a
changing rate into pieces where it is nearly constant, total the pieces. The
only question calculus answers rigorously is what happens when the chopping
gets fine enough to stop being an approximation at all.

The units confirm the meaning, and they always come from the axes. Velocity in
m/s on the vertical axis and seconds on the horizontal axis give an area in m/s
times s, which is meters. Current in amps times seconds gives coulombs. Force
in newtons times meters gives joules. Any claimed integral answer with units
that do not multiply out that way is wrong before a single calculation is
checked.

## Adding rectangles: the Riemann sum

To total a changing rate, cut the interval into narrow slices, treat the rate
as constant inside each one, and add the resulting rectangles. This is a Riemann
sum, and sampling the function's value at the left edge of each slice is the
simplest version. It has a known bias: on a rising curve every left-edge sample
understates the slice, so the sum lands low; on a falling curve it lands high.
Narrower slices shrink the error because there is less room inside each one for
the rate to wander.

Watch it converge on a rising speed v = t^2 between t = 0 and t = 3 seconds:

```python
def v(t):
    return t ** 2                  # speed, m/s

def left_sum(n):
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(n):
        total += v(3 * i / n) * (3 / n)
    return total

print(left_sum(6))
print(left_sum(60))
print(left_sum(6000))
```

The three totals are 6.875, 8.77625, and 8.99775, climbing toward a value this
section will prove is exactly 9. Going from 6 rectangles to 60 cut the error
from about 2.13 to about 0.22; going to 6000 cut it to about 0.0022. Ten times
more rectangles, roughly ten times less error, which is the standard behavior
of this simple method. The estimate is honest, cheap, and improvable to any
accuracy a problem demands, which is more than can be said for guessing.

Sampling at the middle of each slice instead of the left edge kills most of
that bias, because whatever the rectangle overshoots on one side of the
midpoint it undershoots on the other. Midpoint sums and the closely related
trapezoid rule converge much faster for the same effort: cut the slice width in
half and a left-edge sum's error drops by about a factor of two, while a
midpoint or trapezoid error drops by about four. Libraries that integrate
numerically start from exactly these two ideas and add refinement on top.

## The antiderivative: the power rule run backward

The exact total under v = t^2 from 0 to 3 comes from noticing a pattern instead
of adding 6000 rectangles. Differentiating t^3/3 gives 3t^2/3, which is t^2.
So t^3/3 is a function whose derivative is the rate in question, and a function
like that is called an antiderivative. Here is the claim that makes integrals
computable: the exact accumulated total of a rate between two times is the
antiderivative evaluated at the end time minus the antiderivative evaluated at
the start time. For t^2 on [0, 3] that is (3^3)/3 - (0^3)/3 = 9 - 0 = 9, the
number the rectangles were climbing toward.

This is the power rule from the last lesson run in reverse. Differentiating
x^n knocks the exponent down and pulls it out front; integrating x^n does the
opposite, raising the exponent by one and dividing by the new exponent. The
integral of t^2 is t^3/3, the integral of t is t^2/2, and the integral of a
constant c is c times t. One honesty note belongs here: antiderivatives come in
families, because adding a constant to one produces another, since constants
differentiate to zero. The starting value of the problem picks the right
family member. A position integral needs the position at t = 0 to pin down the
constant, which is why every physics problem states where the object started.

The applications are the reason physics and engineering speak in integrals
constantly: their laws state rates, and totals require integration. Distance is
the integral of velocity: integrating 9.8t gives 4.9t^2, recovering the stone's
distance formula from the first lesson, now derived instead of asserted.
Electric charge is the integral of current, since current is charge flow per
second: a steady 2 amps for 10 seconds delivers Q = 2 times 10 = 20 coulombs.
Work is the integral of force over distance: lifting a 100 newton load through
3 meters transfers 300 joules. In each case a conservation law or a definition
supplies a rate, and the integral converts the rate into the total that
actually matters.

A braking car shows the antiderivative doing safety-critical work. A car
slowing from 20 m/s at a steady 4 m/s^2 has velocity v = 20 - 4t, and its
antiderivative is position s = 20t - 2t^2, where the 2 is the deceleration
divided by 2, the power rule dividing by the new exponent once more. The
velocity reaches zero at t = 5 seconds, and the distance covered by then is
s(5) = 100 - 50 = 50 meters. That is the braking distance from 72 km/h under a
firm deceleration, and every yellow-light timing and following-distance rule of
thumb descends from exactly this integral.

## Why practitioners integrate numerically

Everything above assumed a clean formula, and real data rarely comes with one.
A current probe reports a sample every millisecond. A weather station reports a
temperature every minute. A strain gauge on a bridge reports load second by
second. There is no formula to antidifferentiate, only a column of numbers, so
the exact symbolic method has nothing to chew on. The Riemann sum, by contrast,
eats any column of numbers: multiply each sample by the time between samples
and add. That loop is numerical integration, and it is what practitioners
actually run.

Simulators work the same way one step at a time. At each tick they take the
current rate, multiply by the timestep, add the result to the running total,
and move on, which is exactly the meter at the opening and exactly how the
physics lessons ahead predict a dropped ball. The method carries error, and the
error shrinks as the timestep shrinks, at a compute cost that grows the same
way. Take a capacitor charged by a current that decays as i = 2 times
e^(-t/5) amps:

```python
import math

def i(t):
    return 2 * math.exp(-t / 5)    # amps flowing into the capacitor

def charge(step):
    total = 0.0
    t = 0.0
    while t < 10:
        total += i(t) * step       # amps times seconds = coulombs
        t += step
    return total

exact = 10 * (1 - math.exp(-2))
print(round(charge(0.5), 3), round(charge(0.05), 3), round(exact, 3))
```

The exact charge in the first 10 seconds is 8.647 coulombs. The sum with a 0.5
second step reports 9.086, oversetting by about 0.44 because the current is
falling and every left-edge sample catches it at a higher value than its slice
averages. Shrinking the step to 0.05 drops the error to about 0.04. Ten times
finer, ten times better, the same convergence behavior as the rectangles. Skip
the symbolic antiderivative hunt when data is sampled; reach for the sum, and
shrink the step until the answer stops moving.

The same accumulation runs inside hardware nobody calls a computer. A water
meter totals flow rate times time into cubic meters. A battery management chip
totals current over time into amp-hours to report remaining charge. An
inertial navigation system integrates acceleration twice, once into velocity
and once into position, which is why small sensor errors grow into large
position errors the longer the system runs without a correction. Integration
is not a chapter of a math course; it is running in every meter, gauge, and
tracker nearby, in exactly the step-by-step form written above.

<Exercise title="Two rectangles, exactly right">
Take the speed rule v = t over 0 to 3 seconds, whose exact total is 4.5 meters.

Compute the left-edge sum with 6 rectangles by hand: each slice is 0.5 seconds
wide, and the sample speeds are 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5. Add them, get
7.5, multiply by 0.5, and land on 3.75, which undershoots because the speed
rises through every slice.

Now recompute with right-edge samples, 0.5 through 3.0, which gives 5.25 and
overshoots. Average the two sums: (3.75 + 5.25) / 2 = 4.5, exactly right. The
average of left and right sampling is the trapezoid rule, and for any
straight-line rate it is exact, because the rectangles' errors cancel in
pairs. Confirm the three numbers with a five-line Python loop before moving on,
and notice that a rate that curves, like t^2, breaks the exactness and leaves a
small error behind.
</Exercise>
