Trigonometry and Vectors gave every quantity a size and a direction. One
question is still wide open: how fast is a quantity changing at this exact
moment? A trip computer that reports 240 kilometers covered in 4 hours
describes the whole drive at once, and its flat average of 60 km/h is a lie
anyone who has sat in traffic can disprove from memory. The speedometer needle
describes one moment, and the two numbers can disagree by a wide margin because
the average spreads a jerky trip into a smooth fiction. The tool that turns the
first number into the second is the derivative, one of the two big ideas of
calculus.

## Average rate: the slope between two points

The average rate of change over an interval is old friends with a new name:
change in output divided by change in input, the slope from Functions and
Graphs. Applied to position versus time, it is average velocity. A cart that
sits at the 24 meter mark at t = 2 seconds and at the 48 meter mark at t = 6
seconds covered 24 meters in 4 seconds, an average velocity of 6 meters per
second. The units are doing the labeling: meters divided by seconds is a rate
of position, which is what velocity means.

The average is honest arithmetic that hides everything in between. A cart could
have cruised smoothly at 6 m/s the whole time, or stood still for 3 seconds and
blasted 24 meters in the fourth. Both produce the identical average, because an
average over an interval throws away the interval's interior. To know what the
cart was doing at one specific moment, the interval has to shrink.

Real measurements arrive as tables, and the average rate between consecutive
rows is the workhorse move. Say the cart's position reads 0, 6, 24, 54 meters
at t = 0, 2, 4, 6 seconds. The average velocity over the first pair of rows is
(6 - 0) / 2 = 3 m/s, over the next (24 - 6) / 2 = 9 m/s, and over the last
(54 - 24) / 2 = 15 m/s. Three averages from one table, each a slope between two
points, and the growing sequence says the cart is speeding up. Nothing that
follows replaces this move; the rest of the lesson only shrinks the gap between
the two points until the average becomes honest about a single moment.

## Shrinking the interval to an instant

Use the falling stone from the first lesson, whose distance is d = 4.9 t^2.
Between t = 1 and t = 2 it falls from 4.9 to 19.6 meters, an average speed of
14.7 m/s. Between t = 1 and t = 1.5 the average is (11.025 - 4.9) / 0.5 =
12.25 m/s. Shrink further and a pattern locks in:

```python
def d(t):
    return 4.9 * t ** 2           # meters fallen after t seconds

for h in (1, 0.5, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001):
    slope = (d(1 + h) - d(1)) / h
    print(h, slope)
```

The printed slopes run 14.7, 12.25, 10.29, 9.849, then about 9.805. Each
narrower interval reports a smaller average, and the values settle toward one
number: 9.8. That number is the stone's speed exactly at the instant t = 1
second, and it is no accident that it equals g, the gravitational acceleration
in meters per second squared: one second of falling reaches exactly that speed.

Geometrically the shrinking intervals are secant lines whose two anchor points
slide together. In the limit the line touches the curve at a single point and
just kisses it there; that line is the tangent, and its slope is the
instantaneous rate. The derivative of a function at a point is the slope of its
tangent there, defined honestly as the limit of average rates as the interval
shrinks to nothing. Nothing mystical happens in that limit. The averages simply
converge to a single value, as the table shows them doing, and that value is
the answer.

Real instruments never quite reach the instant. A speedometer averages wheel
rotations over a fraction of a second; a GPS unit takes the difference of two
positions a short interval apart. Every physical measurement of speed is an
average over some window, and the derivative is the windowless ideal that those
instruments approximate. When a datasheet claims an instantaneous velocity,
read it as an average over an interval small enough that nobody can tell the
difference, which is exactly the spirit of the table above.

## The derivative of x squared is 2x

The limit can be taken with algebra instead of a table, and the stone's rule is
simple enough to show it. The average slope of x^2 between x and x + h is
((x + h)^2 - x^2) / h. Expand the top: x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2 = 2xh + h^2.
Divide by h: 2x + h. As h shrinks to nothing, the leftover h vanishes and the
slope is 2x, at every x at once. The derivative of x^2 is the new function 2x.

That result generalizes into the power rule, the workhorse of the whole
subject: the derivative of x^n is n times x^(n-1). Bring the exponent down in
front and knock the exponent down by one. Applied to the stone, the derivative
of 4.9 t^2 is 9.8 t, a formula for the speed at any instant: 9.8 m/s at t = 1,
19.6 at t = 2, 29.4 at t = 3. Check those against the differences in the first
lesson's table, 4.9, 14.7, 24.5: those were average speeds over each full
second, and each one sits neatly between the instantaneous speeds at the two
ends of its second, 9.8 and 19.6 for the first row, and so on. The derivative
is the sharp version of the difference table.

Functions in practice carry several terms, and each term differentiates on its
own, with the results added. Differentiating 20t - 4.9t^2 term by term gives 20
and -9.8t, so the velocity formula for the thrown ball arrives with no extra
work. The rule for n = 1 says the derivative of t is 1, which matches the
constant slope every straight line in the first lesson promised. The
derivative of a plain constant is 0, because a constant never changes and zero
is the honest report of its rate; that fact also explains why the constant
families arriving in the next lesson vanish under differentiation.

Worth stating plainly: the derivative is a function, not a number. It assigns a
slope to every point of the original curve, and it can itself be evaluated,
graphed, and differentiated again. Two notations share the job, dy/dx and
f-prime written f'(x), and they mean the same thing.

## Position, velocity, acceleration

Position, velocity, and acceleration form the chain this whole module has been
climbing toward. Position is where something is, in meters. Velocity, the
derivative of position, is how fast the position changes, in meters per
second. Acceleration, the derivative of velocity, is how fast the velocity
changes, in meters per second per second, written m/s^2. Each derivative peels
one layer of "how fast is that changing" off the quantity beneath it.

The falling stone runs the full chain: position s = 4.9 t^2, velocity v = 9.8
t, acceleration a = 9.8, a constant, which is the entire content of the
statement that gravity near Earth's surface pulls with a steady 9.8 m/s^2. The
velocity curve is a straight line climbing at slope 9.8, and the acceleration
curve is a flat line at 9.8, because the rate of the rate has no room left to
change. Running the chain downward, from acceleration to velocity to position,
means undoing derivatives, and that undoing is the integral, the subject of the
next lesson.

## Where the slope hits zero

Maxima and minima are where derivatives earn their salary. A ball thrown
straight up at 20 m/s has a height above the throwing hand of h = 20t - 4.9t^2,
where the negative term is gravity pulling the flight back down. Its vertical
velocity is the derivative, v = 20 - 9.8t. At the top of the flight the ball
stops rising for one instant before falling, and "stops changing" is exactly
what a derivative of zero means. Setting 20 - 9.8t = 0 gives t = 2.041 seconds,
and evaluating the height there:

```python
def h(t):
    return 20 * t - 4.9 * t ** 2    # meters above the hand

def v(t):
    return 20 - 9.8 * t             # vertical speed, m/s

t_top = 20 / 9.8
print(round(t_top, 3), round(h(t_top), 2))
```

The print shows 2.041 and 20.41: the ball peaks about 2.04 seconds after
release, 20.4 meters up. Notice what the method did not need: any knowledge of
where the peak was in advance. Find where the rate is zero, and the peak
announces itself. That trick is not a physics curiosity; every optimization
problem in engineering, maximum power transfer, minimum material for a beam,
fastest route, ends with setting a derivative to zero, which is why derivatives
run half of engineering design.

The same zero-slope test finds minima, not just maxima: at the bottom of a
skateboard ramp the height stops falling and starts rising, and the rate passes
through zero on the way. The ball's full flight also falls out of the same
equation. Setting h = 0 gives landing times of 0 and about 4.08 seconds, and
the peak sits exactly halfway between them, because gravity undoes on the way
down exactly what it did on the way up. One formula, three facts: launch time,
peak height, landing time.

<Callout type="warning" title="A flat spot is not automatically a peak">
A zero derivative means the curve is momentarily level, not that it sits at a
top or a bottom. The curve y = t^3 has zero slope at t = 0 and keeps climbing
straight through; the level spot is an inflection, a change in the direction of
bending. Telling the cases apart needs the second derivative: negative means
the curve is bending downward, so a peak; positive means bending upward, so a
valley. Check before celebrating an optimum.
</Callout>

The derivative chops a quantity into its moment-by-moment rates. The other
half of calculus runs the other way: given the rates, rebuild the total.
Integrals and Accumulation adds up infinitely many tiny slices of a rate, and
it turns out to be the derivative's tool run in reverse.
