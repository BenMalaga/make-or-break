CISA's Zero Trust Maturity Model breaks the work into five pillars, and the reason to care is that it stops you from declaring victory too early. Buy an MFA product and you have touched one pillar. The attacker will find the four you skipped.

The pillars are **identity, devices, networks, applications and workloads, and data**. Three functions cut across all five: visibility and analytics, automation and orchestration, and governance. Think of the five as the walls and the three as the wiring that runs through every wall. A program that is strong on identity and blind on data is not a zero trust program, it is a login page with good marketing.

## Identity is the load-bearing pillar

Everything downstream depends on knowing who is making the request. Weak identity poisons every other control, because a firewall rule scoped to a user is worthless if that user's credential is trivial to phish.

The anchor control here is phishing-resistant MFA, meaning FIDO2 or WebAuthn hardware keys, not SMS codes and not push prompts you can fatigue a user into accepting. Pair it with killing legacy authentication protocols, the ones that predate modern MFA and let an attacker bypass it entirely. If you fix one thing first, fix identity.

## Devices, networks, apps, data

Each of the remaining pillars gets one control worth naming, because a pillar without a concrete enforcement point is a slogan.

- **Devices**: policy decisions read live device posture. Is this laptop enrolled, patched, and running its endpoint agent? Intune, Jamf, and CrowdStrike feed that signal into the access decision, so a jailbroken or unmanaged device fails the check even with valid credentials.
- **Networks**: the network stops being a trust boundary and becomes plumbing you segment hard. Encrypt internal traffic and default-deny east-west flows so a foothold on one host does not reach the next.
- **Applications and workloads**: apps get an enforcement point in front of them and authenticate the workloads that call them, often with mutual TLS between services rather than an open internal API.
- **Data**: classify it, encrypt it, and gate access to the data itself, not just the app that reads it. This is the pillar almost everyone skips, and it is the one that decides whether a breach is an incident or a headline.

Data is last on the list and first to get ignored, which is backwards. The other four pillars exist to protect data. If a compromised app can still read an unencrypted, unclassified dump of your crown jewels, the front-door controls only slowed the attacker down.

## Grade each pillar, do not average them

Maturity is per pillar: traditional, initial, advanced, optimal. The trap is averaging. A program that is optimal on identity and traditional on data does not net out to "medium," because the attacker attacks the weakest pillar, not the mean. Grade each one honestly and spend your next dollar on the lowest wall.

<Callout type="security" title="Test each pillar on authorized systems">
Verifying a pillar means probing it: trying an unmanaged device against a conditional access policy, checking whether east-west traffic is really denied, confirming data stays encrypted at rest. Run those checks against your own lab or infrastructure you have written authorization to assess. Testing another organization's posture without scope is unauthorized access, whatever you call the intent.
</Callout>
