Stress and Materials left the plank standing, and a plank that holds is still
not a machine. Machines begin the moment a scissor jack lifts one corner of a
1,100 kg car: the handle turns with perhaps 150 N of hand force, and the jack
head pushes up with thousands of newtons. Nothing created force out of nothing.
The handle traveled a long distance so the lifting head could travel a few
millimeters. That trade, force for distance, is the heart of this lesson.

## The lever trade

Mechanical advantage is the ratio of output force to input force, and for a
frictionless lever it equals the input lever arm divided by the output lever
arm. The trade is enforced by energy: the work in, force times distance, equals
the work out. Triple the force and the input end must move three times as far.
A lever never gives a free lunch, it gives a favorable exchange rate.

The frictionless part is the ideal. Real hinges, gears, and sheaves shave a few
percent off every machine, so the honest mechanical advantage sits just under
the geometric number, and efficiency is the name for exactly how much of the
ideal survived. Keeping that gap small is most of what lubrication and good
bearings are for, a thread this lesson picks up two lessons from now.

Levers come in three classes depending on what sits between what. In a
first-class lever the fulcrum sits between effort and load, like a seesaw or a
pair of scissors. In a second-class lever the load sits between fulcrum and
effort, like a wheelbarrow or a nutcracker, and this class always multiplies
force. In a third-class lever the effort sits between, like a human forearm,
tweezers, or a fishing rod held mid-shaft; force is lost, but speed and reach
are gained, which is exactly what a throwing arm wants.

Worked numbers: a loaded wheelbarrow presses down with 800 N at 0.45 m from the
wheel axle, and the handles sit 1.35 m out. The lifter needs 800 times 0.45 /
1.35, about 267 N, a mechanical advantage of 3. The same load in a
first-class arrangement with the fulcrum closer to the load could do better,
which is why a pry bar wins arguments with nails.

A crowbar shows the exchange rate at full strength. Press 250 N down on a bar
0.90 m from the pivot with the nail tip 50 mm on the other side, and the nail
sees 250 times 0.90 / 0.05 = 4,500 N. The bar tip sweeps a long arc; the nail
lifts a hair. Long arm on the effort side, short arm on the load side, that is
the whole recipe, and it is why handles, pedals, and crank radii are sized the
way they are.

The third class runs the trade backward on purpose. The human forearm is a
third-class lever with the elbow as fulcrum, the biceps attaching roughly 0.05 m
out, and a load hanging 0.35 m from the elbow. Holding 50 N, about 5 kg, in the
hand demands 50 times 0.35 / 0.05 = 350 N of muscle force, seven times the load.
The body pays in force to buy speed and range, the same bargain tweezers and
fishing rods make: feeble at the tip, quick, and precise.

## Gears multiply by tooth count

A gear is a lever that renews its grip continuously. Two meshed gears exert the
same tooth force on each other at their rims, but a larger gear has a larger
radius, so the same force wraps a bigger lever arm and produces more torque.
Torque scales with tooth count, and speed scales inversely, exactly. A 12-tooth
pinion driving a 48-tooth gear turns the big gear at one quarter of the pinion
speed while multiplying its torque by 4, minus a few percent lost to friction.

The same arithmetic runs every chain and belt drive. A bicycle in low gear puts
the chain on a small front chainring and a large rear sprocket: speed drops,
torque at the wheel rises, and a hill becomes climbable. Racers do the reverse.
Bicycles are the fastest way to develop a feel for gear ratios, because the
trade arrives through the pedals honestly and instantly.

Details fill in the picture cheaply. An idler gear dropped between driver and
driven flips the output direction without touching the ratio, because its tooth
count cancels out of the product. Mesh type is a sound and bearing decision:
spur gears with straight teeth are simple and loud at speed, helical teeth run
quiet but push sideways on their shafts, and bevel gears pass rotation around a
corner. All of them obey the same tooth-count arithmetic.

Rotating parts also need a unit conversion that trips people: revolutions,
radians, and distance. One revolution is 2 pi radians, and a point at radius r
traveling through angle theta covers arc length r times theta. A road wheel of
radius 0.34 m turning 100 revolutions covers 2 pi times 0.34 times 100, about
214 m. This little chain of conversions is what turns a motor's rpm and a
wheel's radius into the speed of a vehicle, and it shows up in the code below.

```python
def gear_train(rpm, teeth):
    # teeth: list of (driver, driven) tooth counts per mesh
    for driver, driven in teeth:
        rpm = rpm * driver / driven
        print(f"{driver} -> {driven} teeth: {rpm:.0f} rpm")
    return rpm

# 1800 rpm motor: 12-tooth pinion into 60-tooth gear,
# then a 15-tooth pinion into a 45-tooth gear
final = gear_train(1800.0, [(12, 60), (15, 45)])
print(f"final speed: {final:.0f} rpm, torque ratio 1800 / {final:.0f}")
```

Two meshes take 1,800 rpm down to 360 and then to 120, an overall speed ratio of
15 to 1. Torque climbs by the same factor 15, less friction, which is why a
small motor through a proper gear train can sound quiet while doing real work.
Gear trains multiply this way stage by stage, and the overall ratio is the
product of the stage ratios.

## Pulleys share the load

A pulley on a fixed bracket changes the direction of a pull and does nothing
else: the mechanical advantage is 1. The useful trick is the movable pulley.
Hang a pulley from the load and run the rope up through it, and the load now
hangs from two rope segments that share its weight. Count the rope segments
supporting the moving part: that count is the mechanical advantage. A four-segment
block and tackle lifting 800 N needs a 200 N pull, and to raise the load 1 m the
puller must haul in 4 m of rope, one meter through each supporting segment.

Real sheaves are not frictionless. Each one wastes a few percent, so the honest
pull for that four-segment tackle is closer to 210 or 220 N, and heavily loaded
systems with many sheaves lose more. Sailors and stage riggers learn to count
segments and then add a margin for sheave friction, because the textbook number
is a floor, not a promise.

Cranes still hang their hooks from multi-sheave blocks, and the reason is not
magic but procurement. Splitting a 50 tonne hook load across eight rope segments
lets eight ordinary ropes share the job instead of one cable carrying it all,
and each pulley sheave adds only a few percent of loss. The winch pays in rope
speed: it must haul eight meters of rope for every meter the hook rises. Force
for rope length, the same exchange as every lever, now done with cordage.

## Cams and linkages

Levers, gears, and pulleys pass rotation and force around. Two more parts change
the type of motion itself. A cam is a rotating lump of shaped metal that pushes
a follower: rotate the lobe and the follower rises and falls on a schedule the
profile dictates. Every engine camshaft does exactly this, opening each intake
and exhaust valve at the right moment and millimeter. A crank and connecting rod
do the reverse trick, turning continuous rotation into a sliding stroke, which
is what turns a piston's up-and-down into the spins that reach the wheels.

A linkage is a set of rigid bars joined by pivots, and it steers motion along
curves no single part could trace. A windshield wiper is a four-bar linkage
turning one motor's rotation into a wide sweep. An excavator's arm is a stack of
them. Designers of mechanisms think in exactly these pieces, primitives that
trade force for distance or reshape motion, assembled like sentences.

Two refinements make cams worth respecting. A cam profile can include dwell, a
stretch of perfect circle that holds the follower still, which is how an engine
valve stays shut for exactly the right slice of each revolution. And a cam can
only push its follower, never pull, so a spring closes the gap or the profile
stays gentle enough that gravity does the closing. The scotch yoke is the
linkage twin of the cam: a pin riding in a slot turns rotation into a pure
sinusoidal slide, a favorite inside vibration testers and reciprocating saws.

<Exercise title="Measure a doorknob">
A doorknob is a wheel and axle, a lever class all its own, and it can be
measured without opening anything. Measure the distance from the center of the
knob to its outside edge, then measure the half-width of the spindle that
actually turns the latch. On a common 55 mm knob driving a 6 mm spindle, that is
27.5 mm against 3 mm.

Divide the two: the mechanical advantage is about 9. Turning the knob edge with
a modest 5 N of fingertip force delivers about 45 N at the spindle surface,
which is why a spring-loaded latch yields to a hand and would laugh at the same
force applied directly to the spindle.

Now the trade: the knob edge travels 9 times farther than the spindle surface
per turn of the wrist. Feel it on the next door, the wrist flick is small and
the spindle barely twitches.
</Exercise>
