The last lesson, Forces and Structures, balanced a worker on a plank and stopped
there. Equilibrium said the supports push back with 530 N and 230 N. It said
nothing about whether a pine plank 50 mm wide can actually take that, or whether
it bends permanently and stays sagged the moment the worker steps off. Whether a
member survives its load is a materials question, and it starts with a division.

## Stress is force spread over area

Hang 10,000 N, roughly a metric ton, from a steel rod, and the danger depends
entirely on how thick the rod is. Stress is the force divided by the area
carrying it, and its unit is the pascal: one newton per square meter. A pascal
is a tiny amount, so real stresses are quoted in megapascals, abbreviated MPa,
each worth a million pascals. Atmospheric pressure is about 101 kPa, which helps
calibrate the scale.

A 10 mm diameter rod has a cross-sectional area of pi times 0.005 squared, about
7.85 times 10^-5 m^2. The stress in it is 10,000 / 7.85e-5, which is 1.27 times
10^8 Pa, or 127 MPa. Shrink the rod to 5 mm and the area falls by a factor of
four, because area scales with diameter squared, so the stress jumps to about
508 MPa, past what mild steel can carry. Same load, same material, one diameter
apart: one rod is fine and the other is in trouble. In a straight pull, area,
not length, sets the stress.

A felt comparison helps: 127 MPa means every square millimeter of the rod's cross
section carries 127 newtons, the weight of a 13 kg mass pressing on each square
millimeter. That is why stress numbers look enormous next to everyday weights.
The same division works in compression. A table leg carrying 400 N on a 30 by
30 mm cross section sees 400 / 0.0009, about 444 kPa, trivial for wood, which is
why furniture legs stay slim. Slender compression members do have a second
failure mode, though: they can bow sideways and collapse, a behavior called
buckling, at loads far below the material's crush strength. A thin rod that
would laugh at 10,000 N of tension can buckle under a few hundred newtons of
compression, which is why the compression members of a truss are fatter than
the tension ties doing comparable work.

## Strain and stiffness

Stress says how hard a material is being pulled or squeezed. Strain says how
much it deforms in response: the change in length divided by the original
length, a plain fraction with no unit. Stretch a 2.0 m rod by 1.2 mm and the
strain is 0.0012 / 2.0, or 0.0006. Engineers say six hundred microstrain.

Over a wide range of everyday loads, strain is proportional to stress: double
the pull, double the stretch. The ratio of stress to strain is Young's modulus,
written E, and it is the stiffness of the material. It comes out in pascals like
stress, because strain has no unit. Steel's E is about 200 GPa, aluminum about
70 GPa, and wood along the grain about 10 GPa. GPa means gigapascals, a billion
pascals.

Run the numbers on the 10 mm steel rod from the last section, 2.0 m long and
pulled with 10,000 N. Stress 127 MPa divided by E gives strain: 127 times 10^6 /
200 times 10^9 = 6.4 times 10^-4. Multiply by the 2.0 m length and the rod
stretches about 1.3 mm. Make the same rod out of aluminum and the strain is 127
times 10^6 / 70 times 10^9 = 1.8 times 10^-3, so the stretch is about 3.6 mm,
more than three times as much. Stiffness and
strength are separate properties. Steel and a good aluminum alloy can have
similar strength, yet steel is roughly three times stiffer, which is why
precision machine frames are steel even when aluminum would carry the load.

Stiffness differences are easy to feel in other materials. Common plastics run
between 1 and 3 GPa, twenty to a hundred times floppier than steel, which is why
a plastic clip springs back visibly while a steel bracket in the same shape
barely moves. Rubber sits at the far bottom near 0.01 GPa, stretching by tens of
percent instead of fractions of a percent. A material's modulus decides how much
a part moves long before strength enters the conversation.

## How materials give up

Below a certain stress, a stretched rod springs back to its original length when
the load comes off. That region is elastic deformation, and it is the region
everyday design lives in. Past a limit called the yield strength, part of the
stretch stays after unloading: plastic deformation. Mild steel yields near
250 MPa. Push further and it keeps stretching, actually getting a little stronger
through strain hardening, until it reaches its ultimate strength somewhere
between 400 and 550 MPa, then necks: one small region thins while the rest stops
stretching, and the break happens there.

Materials split into two failure personalities. Ductile materials such as steel,
aluminum, and copper stretch visibly, neck down, and warn before they fail. A
bent steel nail is ductile behavior on display. Brittle materials such as glass,
cast iron, and concrete in tension snap with no warning at all: a crack forms and
crosses the whole part in microseconds. Glass fails in practice at perhaps
50 MPa even though its theoretical limit is far higher, because every scratch on
its surface concentrates stress at its tip. Wood sits between the extremes,
carrying 40 to 80 MPa along the grain depending on species, and splitting
cheerfully along that same grain when pulled across it.

Two more ideas finish the failure story. Stress concentrates at any abrupt
change in shape: the material flowing around a hole or a sharp inside corner
carries several times the average stress right at the edge, which is why cracks
start at holes and notches instead of in the calm middle of a plate. Rounded
fillets spread that concentration out, and a fillet in the right place is often
the cheapest strength upgrade available. Fatigue, the slow growth of cracks
under repeated loading, is the other quiet killer: a part that survives one
million lifts is not guaranteed to survive two million. Steel has an approximate
endurance limit below which it can last indefinitely, while aluminum has none
and accumulates damage from any repeated stress, which is why aircraft aluminum
lives under an inspection schedule.

The yield strength, not the ultimate, is the number designers plan around for
parts that must keep their shape. A bracket that has gone plastic is a bracket
that no longer aligns anything, even if it is a long way from snapping.

## Factor of safety

No engineer runs a part right at its limit. The factor of safety is the ratio of
the strength to the working stress: design a rod to run at 100 MPa in steel that
yields at 250 MPa and the factor is 2.5. Why 2 or more? Because loads exceed
their estimates whenever anything moves fast or lands hard, because material
properties vary batch to batch, because hidden flaws and corrosion eat margin
over the years, and because repeated loading can crack steel at stresses well
below yield, a subject called fatigue. Each of those alone would justify margin;
together they demand it.

The factor is tuned to how well everything is known. Elevator cables work around
a factor of 8, because a snapped cable is catastrophic and the loads repeat
millions of times. Aircraft structures run factors near 1.5, which sounds
reckless until the other half of the deal shows up: certified materials,
inspections, and weight so expensive that every kilogram is fought over. A
bracket holding a garden hose reel can sit comfortably at 3 or 4, because extra
steel is cheaper than analysis there.

Read the factor honestly in both directions. A factor of 1.2 on a carefully
measured load is braver than a factor of 4 on a guess, because the factor only
multiplies the number that was estimated; if the estimate is off by three, no
factor rescues it. That is why good practice pins down the real load first and
then picks a modest, justified margin, instead of sprinkling safety everywhere
and shipping a heavy, expensive part that still fails where the guess was wrong.

```python
import math

F = 10000.0           # 10,000 N hanging from a steel rod
yield_stress = 250e6  # mild steel yield strength, Pa

for d_mm in (4, 8, 12, 16):
    r = d_mm / 2000.0              # radius in meters
    area = math.pi * r * r
    stress = F / area
    print(f"d = {d_mm:2d} mm  stress = {stress/1e6:6.1f} MPa  "
          f"safety factor = {yield_stress/stress:4.2f}")
```

The 4 mm rod runs at 795.8 MPa, a factor of 0.31: it yields the moment the load
lands and keeps stretching. The 8 mm rod survives elastically at 198.9 MPa but
with a factor of 1.26, one overloaded delivery away from a bent hanger. The
12 mm rod at a factor of 2.83 is the sensible pick. The 16 mm rod is safe and
heavy, paying for margin nobody asked for. That is the whole design conversation
in four lines.
