Power and Machines ended with a drivetrain that works beautifully in numbers.
Then the part gets made, and the shop floor teaches its first lesson fast: the
10 mm motor shaft does not slide into the bracket printed with a 10 mm hole,
because the hole came out at 9.7 mm. Nothing failed. No process on earth
repeats a size exactly, and a design that pretends otherwise fails in the hand,
not on the spreadsheet.

## No part is exact

Every drawing dimension needs a tolerance, the allowed band above and below the
nominal size. A shaft drawn at 10.0 mm with a tolerance of plus or minus 0.05 mm
may legally arrive anywhere from 9.95 to 10.05 mm, and every one of those parts
is a good part. Tolerances are not sloppiness. They are the honest statement of
what the process can hold, and they are what lets two suppliers make mating
parts in different cities that assemble on the first try.

Process decides the band. A well-tuned hobby 3D printer holds about plus or
minus 0.2 mm, and layer lines add their own waviness. CNC milling in a real shop
routinely holds plus or minus 0.025 mm, and precision grinding goes tighter
still. Injection molding lands around plus or minus 0.1 mm for a small part.
Tolerance is bought, never given, and the price is the subject of a section
below. Inspection closes the loop: digital calipers read to 0.01 mm and measure
accurately to about plus or minus 0.02 mm, while a micrometer holds a real
hundredth, which is why a bearing seat gets the micrometer and a bracket gets
the calipers.

Where two parts meet, design for the worst case, not the nominal. The code
below checks a shaft and hole with realistic bands:

```python
shaft, shaft_tol = 10.00, 0.05   # mm
hole, hole_tol = 10.20, 0.05     # mm

tightest = (hole - hole_tol) - (shaft + shaft_tol)
loosest = (hole + hole_tol) - (shaft - shaft_tol)
print(f"worst-case tight fit: {tightest:.2f} mm clearance")
print(f"worst-case loose fit: {loosest:.2f} mm clearance")
```

The worst tight fit is (10.15 - 10.05) = 0.10 mm of clearance and the loosest is
0.30 mm. Both numbers, not the 0.20 mm the nominals suggest, describe the real
product. A designer who checks only nominals is checking a part that will never
exist.

Real assemblies stack the problem up. A bracket bolted to a plate that sits in a
housing inherits the tolerance of every layer between it and the reference
surface, and each part's band adds to the worst case at the far end of the chain.
Designers who care about a final gap count the links, tighten the one or two
that dominate, and leave the rest generous. Checking nominals and hoping is how
assemblies arrive needing a hammer.

## Fits: shaft into hole

Three families of fit cover every shaft-and-hole joint. A clearance fit always
leaves a gap: the largest shaft is smaller than the smallest hole, so the parts
slide, like a bolt through its holes or a shaft spinning in a plain bearing. An
interference fit always overlaps: the shaft is bigger than the hole even at
worst case, so the parts must be pressed or shrunk together and they grip
permanently, like a bearing outer race pressed into its housing, where a few
hundredths of a millimeter of interference at a 10 mm size does the holding. A
transition fit straddles the line: the bands overlap both ways, so one part may
slide and its twin may need a light press, which suits locating dowels that must
not wobble but must come back out.

The fit is chosen by function, and the function usually announces itself. Parts
that must move need guaranteed gap. Parts that must never move need guaranteed
overlap. Parts that must locate precisely and still disassemble live in the
narrow middle, and pay for it in tighter tolerances than either neighbor.

Standards make fits routine instead of bespoke. ISO 286 tabulates standard bands
for shafts and holes by size, and a drawing that calls out a 10 mm running fit
as H7/g6 has told the machine shop everything: both exact bands come straight
off the standard's tables, in micrometers. Nobody derives a fit from first
principles on the shop floor; the lookup is the professional move, the same way
no one re-derives resistor color codes.

## Tighter is not better

Every step tighter on a tolerance costs real money, and the curve bends upward
fast. A plus or minus 0.2 mm band on an milled bracket is nearly free, since any
mill holds it while breathing. Demand plus or minus 0.01 mm on the same bracket
and the shop reaches for a better machine, slower passes, temperature control,
and inspection on every part, and the price multiplies several times over. Tight
tolerances also raise scrap, because more parts fall outside a narrower band.

The professional habit is to specify one generous general tolerance for the whole
part and tighten only where function demands it: a bearing seat, a sliding way,
a sealing surface. A hole that only ever meets a loose M4 screw does not care
about a hundredth of a millimeter, and it should not be billed as if it did.
ISO 2768, the general-tolerance standard, exists precisely so a drawing can say
"all others per standard" and every shop knows what that means.

Shops quote the cost curve in plain terms. Holding plus or minus 0.1 mm on a
milled part is close to the price of the part itself. Halve the band and the
price climbs on a slope, not a step; grind to plus or minus 0.005 mm and the
part visits a machine that exists mostly for grinding, with inspection to match.
Every halving gets paid for twice, once in setup and once in scrap.

## Choosing how to make it

Three processes cover most of what a small shop or product line needs, and each
wins a different race. 3D printing builds parts in hours with almost no setup,
and geometry is nearly free, which makes it unbeatable for one-offs and
prototypes; the trade is strength that varies with print direction and that
plus or minus 0.2 mm band. CNC machining carves strong, accurate parts from
almost any material and holds plus or minus 0.025 mm without strain, but the
machine time is billed by the hour, so the tenth part costs nearly what the
first did. Injection molding turns out parts in seconds for pennies, but the
mold tool costs five figures and weeks of lead time, so it only pays once the
quantity reaches thousands.

That tooling price is where design-for-manufacture earns its keep, because
molded parts must obey rules a prototype never faced. Vertical walls need draft
angles, one or two degrees of taper, or the part fights the mold on every
ejection. Wall thickness wants to be uniform, because a thick region cools
slower, shrinks more, and leaves a sink mark on the show surface. Sharp inside
corners crack; fillets fix them. Designers who run cheap prototypes in the
printed world get ambushed by these rules at molding time unless they design for
the destination process from the start.

Material follows the process. Printed parts arrive as PLA, PETG, or, on better
machines, nylon and carbon-filled blends, with strength that depends on print
direction because the layer interfaces are the weak planes. Machined parts take
whatever the stock is: aluminum 6061, steel, titanium, whatever the job buys,
at full bulk strength in every direction. Molded parts live in engineered
thermoplastics picked for the part's entire service life, and the mold
frequently outlives the product it makes.

The same thinking covers assembly. Standard stock, 6 mm plate, M3 and M4
fasteners, off-the-shelf extrusions, beats custom sizes that need special
orders. Fewer parts beat more parts, and a part that can only go in one way
beats one that can go in backwards.

Under all of it sits one source of truth: the CAD model. The model carries the
geometry that every process downstream consumes, whether that is toolpaths for a
mill or the STL a printer slices, and drawings carry the tolerances and critical
dimensions the model cannot say. Mechanical drawings are almost always in
millimeters, and a dimension without a tolerance is a suggestion, not a spec.
When the model and the part disagree, the model wins the argument, which is why
serious shops refuse to work from anything else.

The first prototype teaches, the second one usually works, and the discipline is
to make the cheap version first: print the bracket, find the interference, fix
the model, print again. The progression is deliberate. Printed parts answer fit
and geometry questions cheaply, machined parts answer strength questions in real
material, and molded parts arrive only after both are settled. Asking a
prototype a question its process cannot answer wastes the round. Iteration is
not a sign of a bad designer; it is the process working as designed.
