A Geographic Information System, almost always shortened to GIS, is software for
working with data that is tied to a place on the Earth. That last part is the
whole point. A list of coffee shops in a spreadsheet is just names and addresses.
The same list in a GIS knows where each shop actually sits, so it can answer a
question the spreadsheet cannot: which shops are within a five minute walk of the
train station, and how many people live close enough to reach one on foot.

The word "system" is doing real work in the name. A GIS is not one tool. It is the
combination of the data, the software that stores and draws it, and the methods an
analyst uses to ask questions of it. People use "GIS" loosely to mean the software
(QGIS, ArcGIS), the field of study, and the whole practice of spatial analysis.
The context usually makes the meaning clear.

## Every GIS record has two halves

The idea that separates GIS from an ordinary database is that every record carries
two kinds of information at once.

The first half is **where**: the location, expressed as coordinates. A single point
might be a pair of numbers like a longitude and a latitude. A road is a string of
such points connected in order. A county is a closed loop of points that encloses
an area. This locational half is called the **geometry** of the record.

The second half is **what**: everything else known about that place. A record for a
fire hydrant might store its geometry (one point) alongside its install year, its
water pressure, and its last inspection date. This descriptive half is called the
**attributes**, and it lives in an ordinary table of rows and columns, exactly like
a spreadsheet. One row per feature, one column per property.

A **feature** is the pairing of the two: one geometry plus its row of attributes.
A hydrant, a road segment, a lake, a census tract. GIS work is almost always work
on features, thousands or millions of them at a time.

<Callout type="info" title="Geometry plus attributes">
Hold on to this pairing, because it explains most of what follows. Every operation
in GIS either works on the geometry (how far apart are these two points), on the
attributes (which parcels are zoned commercial), or on both at once (the total
value of every parcel within the flood zone). Knowing which half a question touches
tells an analyst which tool to reach for.
</Callout>

## Data comes in layers, not one big table

An analyst almost never keeps every kind of feature in a single table. Roads live
in one dataset, rivers in another, buildings in a third. Each of these is a
**layer**: a collection of features that are all the same kind of thing, all sharing
the same set of attribute columns.

Layers are the working unit of GIS. A map is built by stacking them, drawing the
rivers, then the roads over the rivers, then the building footprints over the
roads. Because each layer is separate, an analyst can turn one on or off, restyle
it, or run analysis on it without disturbing the others. This is very different
from merging everything into one giant table, which would force unrelated things to
share columns that make no sense for most of them.

The layer model also mirrors how the questions arrive. A planner asks about roads
and parcels together, then about rivers and parcels, then about all three. Keeping
each theme in its own layer lets the analyst combine exactly the layers a question
needs and leave the rest out.

## The questions a spreadsheet cannot answer

Anything a spreadsheet can do with the attribute table, a GIS can do too, because
that half really is a table. The reason to use a GIS is the other half. Because the
software knows the geometry, it can answer questions that involve location, nearness,
and shape. These are called **spatial questions**, and they are the reason the field
exists.

A few examples make the difference concrete:

- Which homes are inside the 100 year flood plain? A spreadsheet has no idea where a
  home is relative to a flood zone. A GIS checks each home's point against the flood
  polygon and returns the ones that fall inside.
- What is the shortest route for a delivery van that must visit twelve stops? This
  needs the road network's connections and lengths, which live in the geometry.
- How much forest was lost near a new highway between two satellite images? This
  compares two grids of land cover in the same place across time.

None of these can be answered by sorting or filtering a plain table, because none of
the needed facts (inside, shortest, near) are written in any column. They have to be
computed from the geometry. That computation is what a GIS is for.

## Where GIS shows up

GIS is quietly behind a large amount of everyday life, which is part of why it is a
useful skill to have.

**Cities and planning** use it to decide where to put a bus stop, which streets flood,
and whether a proposed building blocks a neighbor's sunlight. **Environmental science**
uses it to track deforestation, model where a wildfire will spread, and measure how a
coastline moves. **Public health** used it to map disease spread long before the phrase
was common, going back to a famous 1854 map that traced a cholera outbreak to a single
water pump. **Logistics and delivery** use it to plan routes, place warehouses, and
promise a delivery window. **Utilities** use it to know which homes lose power when a
particular line goes down.

The common thread is that all of these depend on where things are in relation to each
other, and that relationship is exactly what a GIS keeps track of. Learning GIS is
learning to ask and answer that kind of question with real data.

## A GIS is not a drawing program

A common early confusion is to treat a GIS as a fancier version of a drawing app.
Both put shapes on a screen, so the difference is easy to miss. The gap is that a
drawing program only knows how a shape looks: this line is blue, two pixels wide,
starting here and ending there. It has no idea that the line is a river, how long
the river is in kilometers, or which towns the river passes through.

A GIS knows all of that, because its shapes are anchored to real coordinates on the
Earth and carry their attributes with them. Ask a drawing program for the total
length of every road in a city and it cannot answer, since to it the roads are just
strokes of color. Ask a GIS the same thing and it sums the real world lengths from
the geometry in seconds. The shapes look similar on screen, but only one of them
means anything.

This is also why a screenshot of a map is not GIS data. The picture has lost the
coordinates and the attributes; it is now just colored pixels, the same as any
photo. The living data behind the map, the features with their geometry and their
tables, is the thing an analyst actually works with. The map is only one of many
ways to look at it.

## The road from here

The rest of this module builds the foundation underneath everything above. The next
lesson looks at the two ways a GIS stores the world, points and lines and polygons on
one side and grids of cells on the other. After that comes the part that trips up
almost every beginner: coordinate reference systems, the agreement about what a pair
of numbers actually means on a round Earth. Get those ideas solid and the analysis,
the programming, and the maps that come later all rest on firm ground.

A GIS, then, is not a fancy map program. It is a way of holding data that never
forgets where each record belongs, and a set of tools for turning that location into
answers. Everything else in this track is a variation on that one theme.
