A 9 volt battery reads a clean 9.0 volts across its terminals with nothing
connected, then reads 8.0 the moment a motor starts spinning on it. Nothing
broke. The source sagged, and the amount of sag is not a mystery: it is one
resistor's worth of Ohm's law. Kirchhoff's laws can solve any circuit, but
solving the entire network by hand to answer what one load sees at one pair
of terminals is like mapping the whole city to plan one route. This lesson
builds the shortcut practitioners actually use, and it starts with why
sources behave the way they do.

## Real sources sag

An ideal voltage source holds its stated voltage no matter how much current
is drawn. No physical source manages that, because the energy path inside
every real source has its own resistance, called the internal resistance,
which acts exactly like a small resistor in series with an ideal source. The
terminal voltage is the ideal voltage minus whatever the load current drops
across that hidden resistor.

The numbers are real and worth memorizing in round form. A fresh 9 volt
alkaline battery has an internal resistance of about 2 ohms, which grows as
the battery ages. Draw 100 milliamps and the terminal voltage falls to 9
minus 0.1 times 2, which is 8.8 volts. Draw half an amp and it falls to 8.0
volts. A pair of AA cells does better, a fraction of an ohm each, which is
why AAs power high-drain devices more gracefully than 9 volt batteries do.
A car battery sits around a hundredth of an ohm, which is how it cranks a
starter motor with hundreds of amps while barely sagging. The sag is not a
defect to eliminate; it is physics every source carries, and circuit design
either budgets for it or fights it with regulation.

Internal resistance explains a debugging classic: a circuit that works on a
bench supply, the adjustable lab power supply that feeds electronics on a
workbench, and glitches on a battery is often drawing current pulses the
battery's internal resistance turns into voltage dips. The supply has its
own sag, but milliohms of it instead of ohms.

Internal resistance is also measurable with nothing but a meter and one
known load. Read the terminal voltage with nothing connected to get the
ideal source's value, connect a load that draws a known current, read the
sag, and divide the sag by the current: the result is the internal
resistance. The same measurement tells a story about battery health, since
a depleted cell keeps roughly its printed voltage while its internal
resistance climbs to many times its fresh value. That is why a weak battery
can still read 9 volts on a meter and still refuse to run anything that
draws real current: the meter draws almost nothing, so it sees the ideal
source and none of the sag.

## The other kind of source

Voltage sources get all the attention, but the second ideal source pushes a
set current no matter what voltage that requires. An ideal current source
delivering 10 milliamps will raise its voltage as high as needed to keep 10
milliamps flowing; open-circuit it and its voltage climbs without limit,
which is why real current sources always operate within a voltage range.
Real approximations are everywhere once the pattern has a name: a transistor
circuit that holds 20 milliamps through an LED string while the supply
wanders, a bench power supply running in constant-current mode, and a solar
cell in bright light, which behaves far more like a current source than a
voltage source. The reason this matters for analysis arrives in the next
section: when a network gets reduced, a voltage source is zeroed by becoming
plain wire, and a current source is zeroed by becoming an open circuit.

## The Thevenin trick

In 1883 Leon Charles Thevenin proved a statement that still feels slightly
suspicious: any network of sources and resistors, no matter how tangled,
behaves exactly like a single ideal voltage source in series with a single
resistor, as seen from two terminals. The proof does not matter here; the
recipe does, and it has two steps. First, measure or compute the voltage
across the terminals with nothing connected, which is the Thevenin voltage.
Second, compute the resistance looking into the terminals with every
independent source zeroed: voltage sources become plain wire, current
sources become breaks, and the resistors collapse by the series and
parallel rules. Two numbers replace the whole schematic.

The recipe already ran once without ceremony. The 9 volt battery of the
last section, ideal source plus internal resistance, is its own Thevenin
equivalent: open-circuit voltage 9 volts, Thevenin resistance 2 ohms.
Thevenin's theorem says that statement stays true for any network, however
many parts hide behind the terminals.

Work it on the divider that series-and-parallel designed: a 9 volt source,
a 10,000 ohm resistor on top, a 4,700 ohm resistor on the bottom, tap at the
junction, bottom side tied to the circuit's 0 volt reference, ground. The
tap's open-circuit voltage is the familiar 9 times 4700 / 14700, about 2.88
volts. The Thevenin resistance seen looking into the tap is the two
resistors in parallel, because with the 9 volt source zeroed to plain wire,
the top resistor runs from the tap up to a wire that leads back down through
the bottom resistor: product over sum, 10,000 times 4700 / 14700, about
3,197 ohms. That parallel result is not a coincidence of this example; a
divider's Thevenin resistance is always its two resistors in parallel, a
pattern worth memorizing.

Now the loading warning from two lessons ago becomes arithmetic. Attach a
1,000 ohm sensor to the tap. Through Thevenin glasses the circuit is a 2.88
volt source, 3,197 ohms, and 1,000 ohms in a plain series loop, so the
sensor sees 2.88 times 1000 / 4197, about 0.69 volts. The divider that
promised 2.88 volts delivers less than a quarter of it. No simultaneous
equations were solved; the whole sag was one division.

```python
vin = 9.0
r1 = 10000.0
r2 = 4700.0
vth = vin * r2 / (r1 + r2)
rth = r1 * r2 / (r1 + r2)
print("Vth", round(vth, 3), "Rth", round(rth, 1))

rl = 1000.0
v_loaded = vth * rl / (rth + rl)
r_parallel = r2 * rl / (r2 + rl)
v_direct = vin * r_parallel / (r1 + r_parallel)
print("loaded", round(v_loaded, 3), "direct", round(v_direct, 3))
```

The first line prints the Thevenin equivalent, Vth 2.878 and Rth 3197.3. The
second prints the loaded output computed two ways: once through the Thevenin
model and once by collapsing R2 in parallel with the load and re-solving the
divider from scratch. Both print 0.686, which is the point: Thevenin is not
an approximation, it is the same circuit wearing fewer parts. Designers use
it precisely because a whole family of load questions, 1,000 ohms, 10,000
ohms, an input that comes and goes, each becomes one voltage-divider
division against a fixed two-number model.

<Callout type="warning" title="Zeroing is a thought experiment, not a workshop step">
Turning sources off to find a Thevenin resistance happens on paper. Nobody
replaces a 9 volt battery with a plain wire to measure anything: shorting a
real source is the short-circuit mistake from what-electricity-is, and it
heats wire instead of teaching circuit theory. Compute the zeroed network or,
when the circuit is real and powered, deduce the resistance from two
measurements, an open-circuit voltage and one loaded reading, never by
deliberately shorting the output.
</Callout>

<Exercise title="Thevenin a divider by hand">
Take a 12 volt source, a 1,000 ohm resistor on top, and a 330 ohm resistor on
the bottom of a divider. Compute the open-circuit tap voltage and the
Thevenin resistance at the tap with a pencil, then predict what a 100 ohm
load would pull the tap down to.

Check the arithmetic: the open-circuit voltage is 12 times 330 / 1330, about
2.98 volts, and the Thevenin resistance is 1000 times 330 / 1330, about 248
ohms. The 100 ohm load then sees 2.98 times 100 / 348, about 0.85 volts,
which is a sag of nearly three quarters. If a breadboard and those three
resistor values are within reach, build it and put a meter on the tap loaded
and unloaded. The two measurements should land within a few percent of the
pencil numbers.
</Exercise>

## Maximum power transfer

One more question Thevenin answers at a glance: what load extracts the most
power from a network? The answer is a resistance equal to the Thevenin
resistance. A load much larger than Rth sees nearly the full Thevenin
voltage but passes little current, so power, voltage times current, stays
low. A load much smaller passes plenty of current but chokes the voltage
down. The product peaks in the middle, at an exact match, where the load
voltage is half the Thevenin voltage. For the divider above, a matched
3,197 ohm load takes 0.65 milliwatts, and half of the network's power burns
inside Rth on the way.

Compare the matched load against one ten times larger, 31,970 ohms. The
load now sees 2.62 of the 2.88 available volts and draws only 82
microamps, so it receives 0.21 milliwatts, three times less power than the
match, but the network wastes under 10 percent of its output instead of
half. Power and efficiency pull in opposite directions, and the peak of
one sits at the trough of the other.

Match where it matters and skip it where it does not. Radio-frequency work
lives by matching: an antenna and a receiver are designed around 50 ohms
because a weak signal must be harvested with every microwatt accounted for.
Audio amplifiers do the opposite, with output impedances near zero so the
speakers see a voltage source and almost nothing is wasted in the amp. And
nobody matches a wall charger to a phone: a matched charger would burn half
the energy as heat inside itself. Maximum power transfer is a tool for
signals, not for supplies.

Thevenin's model is built entirely from a source and a resistor, and that is
also its limit: everything in it answers instantly, because resistors have no
memory of what happened a moment ago. Two common parts do have memory, and
they give circuits a sense of time. The next lesson, Capacitors and
Inductors, adds them and turns instant circuits into circuits with a past.
