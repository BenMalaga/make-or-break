A string of old holiday lights goes dark from one bad bulb. Fifty bulbs, one
burned filament, and the whole strand dies, because the current lighting every
bulb travels through each of them in a single line. A house, by contrast, keeps
working when the lamp in one room is switched off; the fridge does not notice.
The difference between those two behaviors is the difference between the two
ways any component can connect, and it is the first question worth asking about
any circuit more complicated than the one-resistor loop that your-first-circuit
built.

That lesson used exactly two parts: a resistor and an LED in one loop. Real
circuits have more parts, and every pair of them relates in one of two ways.
Get the two ways right and a page full of symbols collapses into a handful of
numbers that work out on paper. Get them wrong and every prediction after that
is wrong too.

## One path or many

Parts in series sit end to end on a single path, so one current flows through
all of them in turn. There is nowhere else for the current to go. The LED loop
made this concrete: the 20 milliamps leaving the resistor is exactly the 20
milliamps entering the LED. What the parts share is current. What they divide
among themselves is voltage, which is why the 5 volt supply split into 3 volts
across the resistor and 2 volts across the LED.

Parts in parallel sit side by side, both ends tied to the same two nodes. A
node, from that lesson, is one electrical spot: every point joined by plain
wire is the same node, at the same voltage. Because both ends of each part
land on the same two nodes, parallel parts share one voltage. What they divide
is the current. Two 100 ohm resistors in parallel across a 9 volt battery each
see the full 9 volts, each draws its own 90 milliamps, and the battery
delivers the sum, 180 milliamps.

The test that sorts any two parts is one question: what do they share? If the
same current has no choice but to pass through both, they are in series. If
both sit across the same two nodes, they are in parallel. The drawings can
lie, since two parts stacked vertically on a schematic may still be in series;
the sharing is what counts, not the shape.

## Collapsing the network

A group of resistors can often be replaced by one resistor that behaves
identically as far as the rest of the circuit can tell. That single value is
the equivalent resistance, and finding it is the whole algebra of this
section. Series is the easy case: resistances in series simply add, because
the current fights each one after another. A 1,000 ohm resistor in series with
a 2,200 ohm resistor is 3,200 ohms, always more than the largest part. Across
a 9 volt battery that total draws I = 9 / 3200, about 2.8 milliamps.

Parallel is the case people fumble, so it is worth earning properly. Adding a
parallel path gives current another route, which lowers the total opposition,
so parallel resistance always comes out smaller than the smallest branch,
branch being the name for one parallel path. The rule is that the
reciprocals add, a reciprocal being one divided by the value:

```text
1/R_total = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...      (parallel)
R_total = R1 + R2 + ...            (series)
```

For two resistors the parallel rule shortens to the product over the sum.
A 1,000 ohm and a 2,200 ohm pair give 1000 times 2200 divided by 3200, which
is 687.5 ohms. Two identical resistors in parallel are always half the value
of one, and three are a third. Check the worked pair against Ohm's law and the
sharing makes sense: 9 volts across 687.5 ohms draws about 13.1 milliamps
total, of which 9 milliamps goes through the 1,000 ohm branch and about 4.1
through the 2,200 ohm branch. The sums agree because they must.

Series addition also deserves its physical cross-check. The same 2.81
milliamps that 9 volts forces through 3,200 ohms drops 2.81 volts across the
1,000 ohm resistor and 6.19 volts across the 2,200 ohm one, and the two drops
sum to the full 9 volts. That additivity is not luck: one current passing
two oppositions loses energy at each, and the losses stack. It is also the
seed of the divider coming up next.

A short function does the arithmetic for any length of list:

```python
def equivalent(resistors, mode):
    if mode == "series":
        return sum(resistors)
    inverse_sum = sum(1 / r for r in resistors)
    return 1 / inverse_sum

print(equivalent([1000, 2200, 470], "series"))    # 3670
print(equivalent([1000, 2200, 470], "parallel"))  # 279.1576...
```

Three resistors of 1,000, 2,200, and 470 ohms come to 3,670 ohms in series and
about 279 ohms in parallel, smaller than the smallest single resistor, exactly
as the rule demands. The parallel branch of the function divides one by the
sum of reciprocals, which is the general form of the product-over-sum
shortcut. Same physics, no special cases.

## The voltage divider

Two resistors in series across a source, tapped at the point where they join,
is the voltage divider, and it is the most reused pattern in electronics.
Because one current flows through both resistors, the voltage at the tap is
just the bottom resistor's share of the total drop. With R1 on top (from the
supply to the tap) and R2 on the bottom (from the tap to the negative side of
the supply), the tap voltage is:

```text
Vout = Vin * R2 / (R1 + R2)
```

Worked numbers: a 9 volt battery feeds a 10,000 ohm resistor on top and a
4,700 ohm resistor on the bottom. The same current, 9 / 14700 or about 0.61
milliamps, flows through both, so the tap sits at 0.61 milliamps times 4,700
ohms, which is 2.88 volts. The formula gives the same answer in one step:
9 times 4700 / 14700.

Dividers show up everywhere a circuit needs a voltage smaller than its
supply: scaling a sensor's output into a converter's range, setting a
reference voltage, or turning a knob's position into a number, which is what
a potentiometer is, a divider whose split moves with the knob. The
design runs the formula backward. To turn a 5 volt supply into the 3.3
volts many sensors want, pick a ratio of 0.66, and any pair with that
fraction works: 10,000 ohms on top and 20,000 ohms on the bottom give 5
times 20000 / 30000, which is 3.33 volts. Smaller resistor pairs waste more
current through the divider itself; larger pairs waste less but present a
higher resistance to anything connected at the tap. That is the catch, and it
gets its full treatment two lessons from now: the tap only
holds its predicted voltage while whatever is connected to it draws almost no
current. A load that draws real current pulls the tap down, sometimes a long
way.

## Splitting current the other way

Parallel branches split an arriving current, and the split is worth one
formula. The branch with less resistance takes more current, and the share of
each branch is proportional to the OTHER branch's resistance: a branch of R1
carries I_total times R2 / (R1 + R2). Say 27 milliamps arrives at a node, one
electrical spot where paths meet, and splits into a 100 ohm and a 220 ohm
branch. The 100 ohm branch takes 27 times 220 / 320, about 18.6 milliamps,
and the 220 ohm branch takes about 8.4 milliamps. The two add back to 27,
exactly as charge conservation demands, and the next lesson names that
demand the junction rule. The classic slip is putting the branch's own resistance in the
numerator, which predicts more current through more resistance and is
backwards.

## When one part dies

Series punishes failure. In the LED loop, pull the LED out and the resistor's
current drops to zero too: one open break stops everything, because the
single path is gone. That is the holiday light effect, and it is why cheap
series-wired strings came with the tedious game of hunting one dead bulb
among fifty. In a series string the voltage also redistributes when a part
changes; a bulb that fails short, conducting but not lighting, simply gives
its share of the voltage to the rest.

Parallel shrugs it off. Each branch connects directly across the supply, so
one branch failing or being switched out changes nothing in the others: same
voltage, same current, full brightness. This is why house outlets are wired
in parallel. Every outlet sees the same line voltage, 120 volts in North
America and 230 volts in much of the rest of the world, appliances switch on
and off independently, and each draws exactly the current its own resistance
asks for. The tradeoff is that total current adds up branch by branch, which
is why a circuit breaker trips on the sum: too many parallel appliances, one
hot wire.

<Callout type="info" title="Parallel parts are not interchangeable">
Two LEDs wired in parallel across one shared resistor do not split the current
evenly. The LED with the slightly lower turn-on voltage hogs most of it, runs
hotter, and degrades while its neighbor sits dim. your-first-circuit flagged
this and the rule survives everything in this lesson: each LED needs its own
resistor. Parts that share voltage happily, like the two outlets behind a
wall plate, are a different story from parts that would have to share a
single current.
</Callout>

Equivalent resistance, the divider, and the current split cover a huge
fraction of the circuits that get built, but only the tidy ones: circuits that
are purely series, purely parallel, or cleanly built from blocks of each. A
circuit with two sources and a shared branch is none of those, and no amount
of collapsing cracks it. The next lesson, Kirchhoff's Laws, adds the two
conservation rules that solve any circuit, including the ones that refuse to
collapse.
