A type K thermocouple, the sensor in every kitchen thermometer and 3D
printer hot end, produces about 41 microvolts per degree Celsius. Reading
one degree of change means reading 41 millionths of a volt, a signal any
analog-to-digital converter, the circuit that turns a voltage into a
number, would drown in noise before noticing. The
signal needs amplifying, by about a hundred, and it needs the amplification
to be exact and identical tomorrow. Nothing built so far manages that: the
resistors of Sources and Thevenin Equivalents scale voltages but only
downward, and the capacitors and inductors of Capacitors and Inductors
store and filter but never make anything bigger. The part that does is the
most reused analog component in electronics: the operational amplifier,
op-amp for short, and the story of how it works is really a story about
what feedback buys.

## One chip, enormous gain

An op-amp is a differential amplifier packaged as an integrated circuit: it
measures the voltage difference between two inputs and multiplies it by an
enormous number called the open-loop gain. The classic LM358, a dual op-amp
in an 8-pin package costing pennies, manages a typical open-loop gain around
100,000. Its ancestor, the 741 of the early 1970s, taught a generation of
engineers on the same 8-pin outline. The two inputs are labeled plus and
minus, and the output swings toward whichever input is higher, amplified
past all reason.

"Past all reason" is the honest description. With a 9 volt supply and a gain
of 100,000, an input difference of about 90 microvolts is enough to drive
the output from one rail to the other, rail being the shorthand for the
supply's positive and negative limits. Feed the chip a real signal, where
millivolts are small, and the output spends its life pinned against one
rail or the other, a flat line carrying no trace of the waveform it
was handed. Raw, at open loop, an op-amp is nearly useless as an amplifier.
Every real signal saturates it. That is not a flaw waiting to be fixed; it
is the raw material the next section spends.

## Tame it with feedback

The fix is to stop feeding the chip the raw signal and instead let it watch
its own work. Negative feedback routes a fraction of the output back to the
minus input. Now the chip amplifies not the signal but the difference
between what it is being fed and what it is producing, and any overshoot it
makes arrives at its own minus input as a correction. The behavior settles
into the same loop a driver runs with a steering wheel: notice the drift,
correct, notice the new drift, correct. The output glides where it is asked
instead of slamming into the guardrail.

With feedback in place, two rules make every op-amp circuit solvable by
arithmetic, no 100,000-fold multiplications anywhere. First, the inputs draw
no current to speak of: their input resistances are so high that real
circuits ignore the nanoamps that leak. Second, the output does whatever the
feedback network asks, which in practice means the output moves until the
two inputs sit at the same voltage for every practical purpose. That second
rule is the
open-loop gain doing quiet, enormous labor: since any input difference
multiplied by 100,000 would slam the output, the only stable output is the
one where the difference is nearly zero.

## Building amplifiers that obey arithmetic

The non-inverting amplifier puts the signal on the plus input and feeds a
resistor divider from the output back to the minus input: Rf from output to
minus, Rg from minus to ground, the circuit's 0 volt reference. The output
settles wherever it must to make the minus input equal the plus input, and
the divider guarantees that happens when the output is the input times one
plus Rf over Rg. With Rf = 10,000 ohms and Rg = 1,000 ohms the gain is 11,
so a 0.4 volt input produces 4.4 volts. The gain comes from two resistors,
and resistors come in 0.1 percent flavors that do not care about temperature
nearly as much as a silicon chip does.

The inverting amplifier turns the input through a resistor Rin into the
minus input, feeds Rf from output to minus, and ties the plus input to
ground. The minus input then sits at 0 volts for every practical purpose
without being
connected to ground, a spot called the virtual ground, and the gain comes
out as minus Rf over Rin. With Rin = 10,000 ohms and Rf = 100,000 ohms, a
0.2 volt input produces minus 2.0 volts, flipped upside down and ten times
bigger. The minus sign means the supply must swing below ground, so
inverting stages run from a dual supply, plus and minus rails around 0
volts.

How honest is the two-rules shortcut? Run the check:

```python
a_open = 100000.0        # the chip's open-loop gain
rf, rg = 10000.0, 1000.0
ideal = 1 + rf / rg               # the two-rules answer
beta = rg / (rf + rg)             # fraction of output fed back
actual = a_open / (1 + a_open * beta)
print(ideal, round(actual, 6))    # 11.0 10.99879
```

The exact answer for the closed-loop gain, accounting for finite open-loop
gain, is A divided by one plus A times beta. With A at 100,000 and beta at
1/11, that prints 10.99879 against the ideal 11.0: an error near one
hundredth of one percent. The two golden rules are not an approximation of
the truth so much as the truth with the boring digits shaved off.

Design the thermocouple amplifier from the opening with the same two rules.
A gain near 100 turns 41 microvolts per degree into about 4.1 millivolts
per degree, and the non-inverting circuit delivers it with Rf = 100,000 ohms
and Rg = 1,000 ohms, a gain of 101. One honest limit belongs next to that
plan: the golden rules assume the inputs sit at the same voltage, but a
real chip has an input offset voltage, a small mismatch built into its
input transistors. Cheap parts run a few millivolts of offset, which is a
hundred times bigger than the signal being measured, so precision work
specifies low-offset or self-correcting zero-drift parts instead. Knowing
which rule is being stretched is most of analog design.

## Open loop on purpose, the comparator

The saturation that feedback cures is itself a feature when the goal is a
decision instead of a copy. Leave the feedback off and the circuit is a
comparator: when the plus input sits above the minus input the output slams
to the high rail, and when it drops below, the output slams low. Nothing in
between, ever. Feed a slowly wandering sensor to one input and a fixed
threshold to the other, and the output is a clean digital yes-or-no that a
microcontroller can read. A battery monitor puts a divider's 4.0 volts on
one input and the battery terminal on the other; the output rail tells the
firmware whether to warn. Comparators sit inside analog-to-digital
converters, inside
oscillators that square themselves up, and inside every
light that switches on by itself at dusk.

## What the trade buys

Negative feedback is a deal, and it is worth stating both sides. Handed
over: almost all of the gain, from 100,000 down to whatever the resistors
ask for, plus some speed. Gained: an amplifier whose gain is set by resistor
ratios rather than by a chip parameter that varies several-fold between
copies of the same part and drifts with temperature, along with lower
distortion, because the loop corrects the chip's own nonlinearities the same
way it corrects overshoot. Almost every analog design starts by throwing
away the vast majority of the gain on purpose. Predictability is worth more
than amplification, because gain is cheap and surprises are not.

The speed cost has a number attached. An op-amp's gain and its usable
bandwidth multiply out to a fixed figure called the gain-bandwidth product,
about 1 megahertz for the LM358. A closed-loop gain of 11 leaves about
90,000 Hz of honest bandwidth before the loop runs out of surplus gain and
the response sags; a gain of 1,000 leaves a thousand hertz. Feedback cannot
create speed that the chip does not have, it just spends what exists more
honestly.

The direction of the feedback matters as much as its existence. Feed a
fraction of the output to the minus input and the loop self-corrects. Feed
it to the plus input instead, positive feedback, and the loop self-reinforces:
any move grows, and the circuit snaps hard to one state and stays there,
which is exactly what a Schmitt trigger does to stop a noisy threshold from
chattering. Same chip, opposite wiring, opposite personality.

## Where op-amps live

Sensor front ends are the marquee application, and the thermocouple from the
opening shows why: 41 microvolts per degree times a gain of about 100 is
about 4.1
millivolts per degree, a signal a converter can actually bite into. Audio
preamps lift microphone-level signals a thousandfold with little added
noise. Active filters pair op-amps with the capacitors from two lessons ago
to shape frequency response with no big inductors at all. And every
analog-to-digital converter worth its datasheet has an op-amp or a
comparator somewhere in its front door, conditioning the real world before
it becomes numbers.
