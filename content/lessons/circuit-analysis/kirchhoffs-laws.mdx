Series and Parallel ended at the edge of its own method: collapsing works
while a circuit stays tidy, in clean blocks of one path or side-by-side
branches. A circuit with two sources and a shared branch is not tidy, and no
amount of equivalent resistance will crack it. Two rules crack it anyway, and
they are not even new physics. They are bookkeeping for the two things this
track already established: charge does not vanish, and energy does not appear
from nowhere. Gustav Kirchhoff wrote them down in 1845 as a university
student, and every circuit solved since then, by hand or by software, runs on
them.

## Charge never piles up

The junction rule says that the current flowing into any node equals the
current flowing out. A node where three or more paths meet is a junction, and
the accounting has to balance there. If 12 milliamps arrives and one branch
leaves with 5 milliamps, the remaining branches must carry the other 7. No
amount of resistor cleverness changes the ledger.

The reason is physical, not mathematical. Electrons repel each other, so a
node cannot quietly accumulate extra charge: any surplus pushes back against
more charge arriving within nanoseconds. Steady circuits have no charge
warehouse at the junctions. What comes in has to leave, split among the
branches in proportion to what each branch will pass. A branch is the path
between two nodes, and a two-part circuit has one junction worth checking:
the node between the resistor and the LED in the LED loop. One path in, one
path out, so the two parts carry the same current, which is exactly the
series rule from series-and-parallel. The junction rule is what that rule
becomes when a third path joins.

Written as one
statement, currents into a junction sum to zero, with arrivals counted as
positive and departures as negative. Sign conventions like this one are
half of what the next section needs, so it is worth fixing the habit now:
pick a direction, call it positive, and let the arithmetic keep the books.

## Voltage sums to zero around a loop

The loop rule says that around any closed path, the voltage rises and drops
add to zero. Walk a full lap and the walk ends where it started, at the same
potential, so every climb must be paid for by every descent. A battery climb
of 12 volts has to be balanced by 12 volts of drops somewhere along the same
loop. That is energy conservation wearing circuit clothes: a coulomb of
charge returns from its lap with exactly the energy it started with.

your-first-circuit already measured this law without naming it. The meter
read about 3 volts across the resistor and about 2 volts across the LED, and
the two had to total the 5 volt supply. The lesson called that sum the fastest
sanity check there is. Kirchhoff's loop rule is the reason the check works,
and the reason it catches faults: if the measured drops refuse to sum to the
source, some of the story is missing.

The craft is in the sign discipline. Pick a direction to walk the loop,
clockwise or counterclockwise, and count a voltage rise as positive and a
drop as negative, or the reverse, as long as the choice holds
for the whole loop. Traversing a battery from its minus terminal to its plus
terminal is a rise. Traversing a resistor in the direction the current flows
is a drop, sized by Ohm's law. Walking the loop the other way flips every
sign and multiplies the whole equation by -1, which changes nothing. What
ruins a solution is switching conventions halfway around the loop, so pick
once and keep it.

One full pass with numbers makes the bookkeeping concrete. Take a 9 volt
battery feeding a 470 ohm resistor in series with a blue LED, which drops
about 3.2 volts while conducting, as your-first-circuit noted for blue
parts. The current is the leftover voltage over the resistance: 9 minus 3.2
is 5.8, and 5.8 / 470 is about 12.3 milliamps. Now walk the loop clockwise,
starting below the resistor: a rise of 9 at the battery, a drop of 12.3
milliamps times 470 ohms, which is 5.8 volts, and a drop of 3.2 at the LED.
The sum is 9 - 5.8 - 3.2 = 0. The loop rule closes the books, and any
measured reading that refuses to close them points at a missing piece of
the story.

## Cracking a two-loop circuit

Here is a circuit no divider can touch. Three resistors form an upside-down
U: a 100 ohm resistor R1 along the top left, a 200 ohm resistor R3 along the
top right, and a 100 ohm resistor R2 down the middle, shared by both halves.
A 12 volt source stands on the left edge and a 6 volt source on the right
edge, flipped so that both sources push current clockwise around their own
loops. The bottom rail is common to everything.

Assign a loop current to each loop: I1 clockwise around the left loop, I2
clockwise around the right loop. The middle resistor carries both, in
opposite directions, so its net downward current is I1 minus I2. Drawing an
imagined current around a loop is the step that looks illegal, and the
junction rule is what makes it legal: where two imagined loop currents share
a branch, their contributions add to produce the real branch current, and
everywhere else a loop current is the real one. The alternative method,
nodal analysis, assigns an unknown voltage to each node instead and writes
one junction equation per node; same two laws, different unknowns, and the
choice is about which bookkeeping is shorter. Simulators prefer nodal
analysis for exactly that reason. Now write the loop rule once per loop,
drops matching rises:

```text
Loop 1:  12 = I1*100 + (I1 - I2)*100
Loop 2:  (I2 - I1)*100 + I2*200 = 6
```

Loop 1 says the 12 volt source is spent across R1 and across the middle
resistor. Loop 2 says the 200 ohm resistor is paid for by the 6 volt source
plus whatever arrives, in reverse, through the middle. Simplify both and
solve by substitution, the way any two simultaneous equations fall:

```text
Loop 1:  200*I1 - 100*I2 = 12
Loop 2:  -100*I1 + 300*I2 = 6
From loop 1:  I1 = (12 + 100*I2) / 200
Substitute into loop 2:
  -100*(12 + 100*I2)/200 + 300*I2 = 6
  -6 - 50*I2 + 300*I2 = 6
  250*I2 = 12
```

So I2 = 12 / 250 = 0.048 amps, and back-substituting gives I1 = (12 + 4.8) /
200 = 0.084 amps. The middle resistor carries the difference, 36 milliamps
downward. Check both loops with Ohm's law and the books balance: loop 1 spends
8.4 volts on R1 and 3.6 on the middle resistor, totaling the source's 12.
Loop 2 spends 9.6 volts across the 200 ohm resistor, covered by 6 from its
own source and 3.6 arriving backward through the middle branch. One check
more, from the nodes: the top of the middle branch sits 12 volts minus 8.4,
that is 3.6 volts above the bottom rail, which is exactly the drop the
middle resistor must show. Every number agrees with every other number,
which is what a correct solution looks like.

```python
# Loop 1: 200*I1 - 100*I2 = 12
# Loop 2: -100*I1 + 300*I2 = 6
i2 = 12 / 250                    # from -6 - 50*I2 + 300*I2 = 6
i1 = (12 + 100 * i2) / 200       # back-substitute into loop 1
print(i1 * 1000, i2 * 1000, (i1 - i2) * 1000)
```

The print shows the three branch currents in milliamps: 84.0, 48.0, and 36.0.
The same substitution would work for ten loops and a hundred resistors; the
equations just stack higher, and nobody solves a hundred of them by hand.

One sign convention deserves its own warning, because it alarms everyone the
first time. If a solved current comes out negative, nothing failed. The minus
sign means the actual current flows opposite the assumed direction.
The magnitude is correct, the arrow was not, and flipping the arrow finishes
the job. Negative results are information, not errors.

## Why this is machine work

Look at what the last section actually did. Label currents, write one loop
equation per loop with a fixed sign convention, solve the stack of
equations. Nothing in the recipe needs judgment, which makes it a job for a
program. Circuit simulators do exactly that, at scale: SPICE, the
Simulation Program with Integrated Circuit Emphasis first released from
Berkeley in the early 1970s, reads a netlist, the machine-readable list of
every part and connection, stamps out one equation per unknown using the
nodal form, and solves the whole system numerically. Every simulator since,
from LTspice on a laptop to the browser tools used in this track's
projects, is running Kirchhoff's two laws thousands of times per analysis,
with matrix algebra doing the substitution step at machine speed.

<Callout type="tip" title="When the books refuse to balance">
A hand solution that will not close, drops that miss the source by a couple
of volts, is almost always a sign flipped somewhere in one loop equation,
not a physics problem. Recheck the direction of every battery traversal
before doubting the arithmetic. The second most common culprit is a resistor
value typed from a color band read upside down, which turns a 4,700 ohm
part into a 47,000 ohm one and still produces a plausible-looking wrong
answer.
</Callout>

That mechanical nature is also the honest limit of hand analysis. Two loops
solve on one page. Ten loops solve in a simulator in milliseconds and by
hand in an afternoon nobody wants to spend. The skill worth building is
knowing which three or four loops matter for a design decision, and letting
software handle the rest.

The two laws also set up the shortcut this course takes next. Most design
questions do not involve a whole network; they involve what one load will
see at one pair of terminals. Kirchhoff's laws guarantee that the entire
mess behind those two terminals can be summarized completely, and Sources
and Thevenin Equivalents shows the summary that makes a loaded divider a
one-glance problem.
