You can spend a week hardening a network and still get owned by one phone call.
Every technical control on the perimeter has a human standing behind it who can
be persuaded to open a door. That person is the attack surface this module is
about. They do not have a CVE, they cannot be patched on a schedule, and they
show up to work tired, busy, and eager to be helpful.

## Why people are the softest target

Software behaves the same way every time. People do not. A locked-down server
will refuse an unauthenticated request forever, but a help desk operator who is
three tickets behind will reset a password for someone who sounds panicked and
official. Attackers know this. Social engineering skips the exploit chain and
asks the human to hand over what the exploit would have taken.

- It scales cheaply. One convincing email template can reach ten thousand inboxes.
- It sidesteps controls. Multi-factor auth means little if the target types the
  code into a fake login page for you.
- It is hard to log. There is no packet capture of a persuasive conversation.

## The principles of influence

Most social engineering is applied psychology. Robert Cialdini named six levers,
and attackers pull them constantly:

- **Authority**: people comply with someone who seems to be in charge. A vest, a
  badge, or an email signature that reads "IT Security" does most of the work.
- **Urgency and scarcity**: a deadline shuts down careful thinking. "Your account
  will be locked in ten minutes" pushes the target to act before they verify.
- **Social proof**: "everyone on your team already did this" lowers resistance.
- **Reciprocity**: a small favor first makes the target feel they owe one back.
- **Liking**: we say yes to people we find friendly and similar to us.
- **Commitment**: once someone agrees to a small ask, a bigger one feels consistent.

<Callout type="security" title="Why the hunter cares">
Before an attacker crafts a single email, they pick the lever. A pretext that
combines authority (a message from "the CFO") with urgency (a wire that must go
out today) beats a generic request every time. When you review a suspicious
message, name the lever it is pulling. That is the fastest way to see through it.
</Callout>

## The attack cycle

A real engagement is not one message. It is a loop:

1. **Reconnaissance**: gather names, roles, tone, and habits from public sources.
2. **Pretext**: build a believable story and identity to justify the request.
3. **Engagement**: make contact and pull the influence levers.
4. **Exploitation**: get the credential, the click, or the physical access.
5. **Exit**: leave cleanly so the target does not raise an alarm.

The rest of this module walks each stage. Learn the attacker's playbook and you
learn exactly what your own users need to defend against.

<Exercise
  id="soft-target"
  prompt="In one sentence, why does multi-factor authentication not fully protect a user from social engineering?"
>
MFA only checks that a code was entered, not why. If an attacker convinces the
user to read the code aloud or type it into a fake page, the second factor is
handed over just like the password.
</Exercise>
