Phishing is a fraudulent message that gets a target to click a link, open a file,
or hand over a secret. Pretexting is the story that makes the message believable.
One is the delivery, the other is the script. Strong operators write both, and
strong defenders learn to take both apart.

## The phishing family

The word "phishing" covers a spread of techniques that differ mostly by target
and channel:

- **Phishing**: mass, generic email blasted to thousands. Low effort, low hit rate.
- **Spear phishing**: tailored to one person or a small team using details you
  researched. Far higher success.
- **Whaling**: spear phishing aimed at executives, where one catch is worth a lot.
- **Smishing**: the same play delivered by SMS, where short URLs hide the domain.
- **Vishing**: voice phishing over the phone, where urgency and authority land hard.

## Building a pretext

A pretext is a role plus a reason. It must give the target a plausible identity to
picture and a plausible motive to act. The best pretexts borrow the organization's
own routine so nothing feels out of place.

- Match the channel people already expect: an IT reset really does come by email.
- Reference something real: a project name, a vendor, a recent all-hands.
- Keep the ask small and specific. "Confirm your login" beats "send me everything."
- Give an exit that seems normal, like a link to a page that just says "Thanks."

<Callout type="warning" title="Authorization is the line">
Everything in this lesson is for engagements you have written permission to run.
Sending a phishing email to someone who has not authorized the test, or targeting
an organization you were not hired by, is a crime in most jurisdictions. Scope and
rules of engagement come first, always.
</Callout>

## Tooling the campaign

On an authorized assessment, a framework tracks who opened, who clicked, and who
submitted credentials so you can report real numbers. GoPhish is the common
open-source choice.

login screen, an email template, and a target group, then launch and watch the
timeline fill in.

## Reading the red flags

Whether you are crafting or catching, the tells are the same. Train your eye on:

- **Sender mismatch**: the display name says "IT Support" but the address is a
  lookalike domain like `micros0ft-support.com`.
- **Urgency and threats**: "act in 15 minutes or lose access."
- **Hovered link does not match**: the visible text says one site, the URL another.
- **Unexpected attachments**: an invoice or resume you never asked for.
- **Generic greeting** on a message that should know your name, or the reverse:
  eerily specific detail meant to build false trust.
