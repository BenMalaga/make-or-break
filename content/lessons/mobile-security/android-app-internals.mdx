Rename `app.apk` to `app.zip`, unzip it, and you are already inside. An APK is a ZIP archive, nothing more, and the first thing worth internalizing is that everything the app is made of ships in that one file: the compiled code, the resources, the native libraries, and the manifest that declares what the app can do.

The pieces you care about:

- **AndroidManifest.xml** the app's declaration of components, permissions, and entry points. This is the map of the attack surface.
- **classes.dex** the compiled Dalvik bytecode. One or more `.dex` files hold all the Java/Kotlin logic.
- **resources.arsc and res/** compiled resources and layouts.
- **lib/** native `.so` libraries per CPU architecture.
- **assets/** raw bundled files, sometimes including config, models, or embedded secrets.
- **META-INF/** the signature that proves who built it.

The manifest ships in a binary XML format, so `cat` gives you garbage. Decode it with `apktool`, then read code with `jadx`, which turns the DEX back into readable Java.

## The four components, and the word "exported"

Android apps are built from four component types, and any one of them can be an entry point if it is exposed to other apps.

- **Activities** the screens.
- **Services** background workers.
- **Broadcast Receivers** handlers that wake up on events like boot or SMS.
- **Content Providers** structured data interfaces, usually backed by SQLite.

The attribute that decides whether an outside app (or `adb`) can poke a component is `android:exported`. An exported Activity can be launched by anyone. An exported Content Provider can be queried by anyone, and a badly written one hands over the app's private database to any app on the phone. Grep the manifest for `exported="true"` and for `intent-filter` blocks (which imply exported on older targets), and pay special attention to deep-link schemes, because those are attacker-controlled input paths straight into the app.

<ExpectedOutput label="a risky exported provider in the manifest">
&lt;provider android:name=".DataProvider" android:authorities="com.target.app.data" android:exported="true" /&gt;
</ExpectedOutput>

While you are in the manifest, flag two more settings. `android:debuggable="true"` in a production build lets you attach a debugger and `run-as` into the app's data without root. `android:allowBackup="true"` lets you extract the app's private files over ADB with `adb backup`, no root needed.

## Where the data lives

Every app runs under its own Linux UID, and its private files sit in `/data/data/<package>/`, readable only by that UID. That directory is the whole point of the sandbox, and it is exactly what you want to see.

- **shared_prefs/** XML key-value files, the number one home of tokens and flags stored in plaintext.
- **databases/** SQLite files, often unencrypted.
- **files/ and cache/** whatever the app wrote directly.

On a rooted device or emulator you `adb shell` and `cd` straight in. On a debuggable build you do not even need root: `adb shell run-as <package>` drops you into the sandbox as the app. Pull the whole tree and read it.

<CommandCard command="adb shell run-as com.target.app cat shared_prefs/prefs.xml" explain="On a debuggable build, run-as executes as the app's UID so you can read its private storage without rooting the device. This is the fastest way to catch a plaintext auth token." />

Historically apps also dumped data onto external storage (`/sdcard/`), which was world-readable and a reliable leak. Scoped storage tightened that, but old apps and sloppy new ones still write sensitive files where any other app can read them.

<Callout type="security" title="Own the device and the app">
Pulling an APK apart, rooting an emulator, and reading `/data/data` are all fine on hardware you own with an app you are cleared to test. Extracting another person's app data off their phone, or reversing a paid app to redistribute it, is a different act with real legal weight. Keep it to your own builds and the OWASP MAS test apps.
</Callout>
