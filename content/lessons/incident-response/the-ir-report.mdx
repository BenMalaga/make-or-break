The response is over, the box is rebuilt, everyone wants to go home. The report
is the only part of the whole incident that outlives it. It is what the next
responder reads, what leadership uses to fund the fix, and what legal reaches for
if this turns into a lawsuit or a breach notification. A sloppy report wastes the
entire response.

Write it for two readers at once. Executives need the impact and the ask in the
first half page. Engineers need the technical detail to actually close the gap.
Lead with a short executive summary, then go deep, so each reader finds their
altitude without fighting the other's.

## The four sections that carry the report

**Timeline** is the spine. Every confirmed event with a UTC timestamp, in order,
from the attacker's first action to your last recovery step. This is where all
those timestamped log lines and case-note entries pay off. If you kept the case
log honestly during the response, the timeline is mostly assembly, not writing.

**Impact** states what was actually affected in plain terms: which systems, which
accounts, how many records, whether data left the building, and the dwell time
from first compromise to detection. Say what you confirmed and, separately, what
you could not rule out. Never inflate and never soften.

**Root cause** is the one honest sentence about how this happened. Not the
malware name, the underlying failure: an unpatched internet-facing service, a
user who entered credentials on a phishing page, a missing MFA control. The
symptom is the ransomware. The root cause is what let it run.

**Recommendations** are specific, assigned, and dated. Not "improve security."
Instead: "enforce MFA on the VPN by 2026-08-15, owner IT infrastructure." Each
one should trace directly back to something in the timeline or root cause, and
each one feeds the preparation phase of the next lifecycle.

## Build the timeline from artifacts, not memory

If you ran a full forensic collection, tools stitch your disk and log artifacts
into one sorted super-timeline so you are not hand-copying timestamps. Plaso is
the standard.

Even without full forensics, keep the format the same: one line per event,
`UTC timestamp, source, host, actor, action`. A timeline anyone can sort and
audit beats a paragraph of prose describing roughly what happened.

## Drive root cause past the first answer

The five-whys keeps you from stopping at the symptom. Ransomware ran. Why? A user
opened a macro. Why did the macro run? Macros were not blocked. Why was the
attacker on the network at all? A phished credential with no MFA. Why did that
credential reach a server? Flat network, no segmentation. Four whys in, you have
three real fixes instead of one useless "user clicked a bad link."

Watch the tone while you write. The report documents system and process
failures, not people to blame. "The account had no second factor" is actionable.
"The user was careless" ends the investigation and teaches nobody anything.

<Callout type="tip" title="If it is not in the timeline, do not claim it">
Every statement in the impact and root-cause sections should trace to a specific
artifact: a log line, an event ID, a captured file hash. When someone challenges
a claim six months later, you point at the evidence, not your recollection. Claims
without artifacts are the fastest way to lose a report's credibility.
</Callout>

<Callout type="security" title="Handle the report like evidence">
An incident report names real victims, real systems, and often real attacker
infrastructure. Share it only with people authorized to see it, store it with the
case evidence under the same access controls, and follow your legal team's lead on
retention and breach-notification obligations. The report is sensitive material,
treat it that way.
</Callout>
