A user forwards you an email at 2am. Their laptop is acting weird, a finance
approval went out that nobody remembers sending, and three machines are now
beaconing to an IP in a country you do not do business with. Start clicking
around without a plan and you will tip off the attacker, trample the evidence,
and still not know how deep it goes.

The fix is a lifecycle you run the same way every time, whether it is one
phishing click or a full ransomware detonation. SANS calls it **PICERL**. NIST
SP 800-61 folds the same work into four phases, but the six-step breakdown maps
cleaner to what your hands actually do.

## The six phases

**Preparation** is everything you do before the pager goes off: the runbook, the
contact list, tested backups, logging that is actually turned on and retained.
This is the only phase you control the clock on, so it is where most of the real
win lives. An org with no preparation does not respond to incidents, it reacts to
them.

**Identification** is confirming that an event is actually an incident, not a
false positive, and figuring out its scope. One alert is an event. An attacker
with valid credentials moving between hosts is an incident. Getting this call
right and fast is the next lesson.

**Containment** stops the bleeding. You isolate the affected hosts so the
attacker cannot spread or exfiltrate more, while being careful not to destroy the
volatile evidence sitting in RAM. Short-term containment buys you minutes,
long-term containment (rebuilt segments, rotated credentials) buys you the rest
of the response.

**Eradication** removes the attacker's foothold: the malware, the web shell, the
rogue account, the persistence mechanism. If you skip straight here without
understanding how they got in, they walk right back through the same door.

**Recovery** brings clean systems back into production and watches them closely.
You restore from known-good backups, validate the host is actually clean, and
monitor for the attacker trying to return. Rushing recovery is how a
one-weekend incident becomes a three-week one.

**Lessons learned** closes the loop within about two weeks while memory is fresh.
What worked, what was slow, what detection gap let this happen, and which of
those fixes feeds straight back into preparation. Skip this and you will
respond to the identical incident next quarter.

## Start the case before you start the work

The first thing you create is not a fix, it is a record. Open a case folder and
lock the clock to UTC so every artifact you collect lines up on one timeline.

<CommandBlock
  title="analyst@ir-jumpbox"
  commands={[
    { cmd: "date -u +\"%Y-%m-%dT%H:%M:%SZ\"", comment: "note the incident open time in UTC, always UTC" },
    { cmd: "mkdir -p ~/cases/INC-2026-0042/{evidence,notes,timeline}", comment: "one folder per incident, structured from minute one" },
    { cmd: "echo \"$(date -u +%FT%TZ) case opened, source: user report of finance fraud\" >> ~/cases/INC-2026-0042/timeline/timeline.log", comment: "first timeline entry" },
  ]}
/>

Every action you take from here gets a timestamped line in that log. When the
report comes due, or when legal asks what you touched and when, that file is the
difference between a defensible response and a guess.

<Callout type="info" title="PICERL vs NIST">
NIST SP 800-61 lists four phases: Preparation, Detection and Analysis,
Containment/Eradication/Recovery, and Post-Incident Activity. It is the same
work. Pick whichever vocabulary your team documents in and stay consistent, the
map matters less than everyone running the same route.
</Callout>

<Callout type="security" title="Stay inside your authority">
Responding to an incident is not a license to touch anything you want. Isolate,
image, and investigate only the systems inside the scope your organization or
client authorized. Reaching into a system you were not cleared for turns a
responder into a second incident.
</Callout>
