You just pulled a password out of a cleartext stream. Now flip the perspective.
As the attacker on the wire, what can you actually read depending on how the
victim protects their traffic? The answer changes at each layer, and knowing
exactly what stays visible tells you where an attack is still possible.

## Plain HTTP: everything is readable

Over HTTP, there is no encryption at all. An on-path observer, someone sniffing
the same network segment, sees the whole request and response in the clear:

- The destination IP and port (that is always visible, at every level).
- The full URL, including path and query string.
- Every header, including cookies and basic-auth credentials.
- The entire body, including form fields and API keys.

This is the FTP and HTTP-form situation from the last lesson. Sniff the segment,
follow the stream, read the secrets. Plain HTTP gives the attacker everything.

## HTTPS: the payload goes dark

HTTPS is HTTP wrapped in **TLS**. TLS encrypts everything above the transport
layer, so the observer's view collapses to almost nothing useful:

- **Hidden:** the URL path, all headers, cookies, the request and response
  bodies. The password in a POST form is now ciphertext.
- **Still visible:** the destination IP and port, the rough size and timing of
  the traffic, and historically the server name via the TLS SNI field during
  the handshake.

That last point matters. TLS protects the **content**, not the **fact** that a
connection happened. An observer still learns which server you are talking to by
IP, and often by hostname, even though they cannot read a byte of the exchange.

<Callout type="warning" title="HTTPS is not invisibility">
A common mistake is assuming HTTPS hides where you went. It hides what you sent
and received. The destination IP is on every packet by necessity, because
routers need it to deliver the traffic. Metadata leaks even when payload does
not.
</Callout>

## VPN: move the visible endpoint

A VPN wraps all of your traffic in another encrypted tunnel to a VPN server, and
only there does it exit onto the normal internet. That shifts what a local
observer can see:

- Your local eavesdropper, the coffee-shop network or a sniffing colleague, now
  sees only encrypted packets going to one IP: the VPN server. They lose the
  per-site destinations that even HTTPS left exposed.
- The visibility does not vanish, it **moves**. The VPN provider, and any
  observer past the VPN exit, sees your real destinations again. If those sites
  are plain HTTP, that observer reads the payload too.

So a VPN changes **who** can see your traffic, not whether it can be seen at all.
You have traded trust in the local network for trust in the VPN operator.

<Callout type="security" title="Attacker's takeaway">
Layer by layer, your job is to know what leaked. Plain HTTP hands you payloads.
HTTPS still hands you destination IPs, timing, and often the hostname, which is
plenty for traffic analysis and target mapping. A VPN concentrates all of that
into one tunnel endpoint, so your attack surface shifts from the wire to the
endpoints and to whoever runs the exit.
</Callout>
